Reading Skill 1 · Working out unfamiliar words
How to work out the meaning of unfamiliar words

When you are reading, you may not understand every word on the page. However, you can work out the meaning by looking at the context — the words that come before and after the unfamiliar word — and the overall meaning of the text. You can also use your general knowledge and logic to infer meaning.

Skills builder — 4 steps

① Read the text to understand its overall theme and the main idea of the paragraph.
② Read the sentences before and after the word — note whether the tone is positive or negative.
③ Identify the part of speech of the unfamiliar word.
④ Use general knowledge and logic to work out which meanings would be likely in the context.

🚀 Booster

To familiarize yourself with the theme of this unit, go to pages 24–25 for theme-related vocabulary and warm-up questions. The main parts of speech: noun, pronoun, adjective, determiner, verb, adverb, preposition, conjunction, interjection.

Worked Example · Peer pressure

Most teenagers admit that it is peer pressure to be 'cool' that forces them to do certain things. Often teenagers will feel compelled to do something because their friends tell them to do it. However, it is not always as obvious as that. Sometimes teenagers feel pressurized because of the way others behave or talk, even if they don't directly tell them what to do. Sometimes the outcomes of peer pressure will be innocent, safe and maybe even fun. However, the danger comes when peer pressure leads you to engage in risk-taking behaviour, which can lead to damaging results.

🔍 Inference breakdown — click each blank to reveal

• peer pressure → the relative clause tells us: feeling ____.
• compelled → the reason given ("friends tell them to") suggests ____.
• pressurized → you know "pressure"; in this context it means ____.
• risk-taking behaviour → "However" contrasts with safe/fun outcomes, and "danger/damaging" are negative → it means ____.

A1 · Read the article

Powerful pet help for teenagers

¶1
Adolescence and the teenage years can be difficult times; many young people feel under peer pressure and develop stress related to school and examinations. This can lead to depression and low self-esteem. However, keeping and caring for a pet can greatly improve a teenager's emotional and mental health. In times of pressure, when a teenager may worry about whether their friends like them or not, there is great benefit from the unconditional love that comes from a pet, such as a puppy who jumps up excitedly when you arrive home, or a cat who curls up on your knee while you watch TV. The emotional bond between a pet and its owner grows quickly.
¶2
As attachment grows between a pet and its owner, the animal also develops a strong sense of loyalty. Pets will always show a preference for the person who treats them kindly, and will always care for that person. This loyalty can greatly benefit young people during difficult or stressful times. While arguments with friends, punishment from parents or difficulty at school can cause emotional turbulence, a loyal and loving dog who clearly loves that teenager can have a great calming influence on them, and make life's challenges easier to bear.

Reading strategy

① Theme: pets & teenagers' mental health.
② Underline unfamiliar phrases: low self-esteem / unconditional love / emotional bond / loyalty / emotional turbulence / calming influence.
③ Right-click to highlight the context clues around each phrase.

🔍 Positive or negative?

¶1 starts negative (stress, depression) then turns positive after "However". ¶2 is positive (loyalty, calming influence) with a negative contrast ("While arguments …"). Tone shifts help you infer meaning.

Reading Skill 2 · Reference words
Identifying what a reference word refers to

Pronouns, adverbs and noun phrases are often used as reference words — they talk about something or someone already mentioned in the text. Exam questions often test this ability.

Skills builder — steps

① Locate the reference word — pronouns (she, it, them), adverbs of place (here, there), adverbs of time (now, then), noun phrases (the lucky boy).
② Read the sentence containing the word and work out its meaning.
③ Look at nearby sentences for the noun or noun phrase it refers to.
④ The answer must match in number (singular/plural), gender and person. Numbers can also be pronouns: only twenty showed up = twenty celebrities.

Worked Examples · Pronouns & adverbs

Example 1 · close referent

My friend has taken up a new hobby. She loves practising it.

She → My friend  ·  it → a new hobby

Example 2 · referent further away (read the whole paragraph)

I could see 40 friendship bracelets. The colours and patterns were amazing. I asked myself, 'When did she find the time to make all of these?'

My best friend went to Cambodia in 2010. She saw how hard life was for people there and she decided to become involved in humanitarian work. Since then, she has been on missions to more than 30 countries in the developing world.

🔑 地点副词 there/here 回指地名；时间副词 then/now 回指时间名词。

Text 1 · Article ¶1–¶5

Dealing with overprotective parents

¶1
Sarah has very loving parents, and as a little girl this made her feel safe and protected. Her parents have lots of rules for her and she follows them because she believes her parents are correct. However, now that Sarah is a teenager, she wants to go with her friends to the cinema, go camping and do lots of other fun activities that other kids do. Sarah's parents don't let her. They don't want her to leave the house without them; they don't let her meet friends they don't know. Sarah's parents are overprotective.
¶2
Overprotective parents set lots of rules and restrictions that may prevent their children from having the freedom they want—freedom to meet friends, take part in activities and be a regular teenager. Behaviour like this can leave a young person feeling isolated, and can have a very negative impact on their self-esteem.
¶3
What can you do if you have overprotective parents? Here are some helpful ideas.
¶4 Start by following the rules
Even though you don't agree with them all, you need to show that you can follow your parents' rules. This will help them trust you.
¶5 Talk to your parents
Your parents are protective because they love you and want to keep you safe and happy. Try to talk to them about how the restrictions on your behaviour make you feel, and why you want to have more freedom.

Structure

¶1–¶2: the problem (Sarah's story → definition of overprotective parents).
¶3: transition question.
¶4–¶10: SEVEN pieces of advice, each with a bold heading.

🔍 Reference watch

Q1/Q4 both ask about 'them' — but in different lines! ¶1 line 2 'them' = the rules; ¶4 line 13 'them' = the rules too, but check which noun phrase is closest + logical.

Text 1 · Article ¶6–¶10

Dealing with overprotective parents (cont.)

¶6 Don't push too far too quickly
You shouldn't try to break any of the big rules, and don't try to persuade your parents to let you do anything too radical. If they don't want you to go out in the evening, don't stay out till midnight the first time they let you go.
¶7 Reassure them with things that they want
If your parents ask you to send a text message when you arrive at your destination, make sure you do it, and why not send an extra one to say thank you for letting you go out?
¶8 Build trust between your parents and your friends
Many parents think that their children's friends are not good for them and are a bad influence. Invite some of your friends over (one or two at a time), so your parents can meet them and learn to like and trust them.
¶9 Be patient, keep trying
Your parents have been protecting you for a long time, and they have been setting rules for you for a long time too, so they may not accept changes very quickly. They also won't want to feel pressurized by you constantly asking them. You need to wait, take your time, be patient and try again until they start to give you some freedom.
¶10 Be determined, put your foot down
Eventually, you may have to confront your parents. If this happens, it is important that you talk about how their rules make you feel, and how now that you are older, you really need more freedom. You don't want to make them feel guilty, but you do want them to be motivated to make changes.

Key vocabulary to hunt

• radical (¶6) — "don't stay out till midnight the first time" → extreme
• reassure (¶7) — make parents feel less worried
• bad influence (¶8) — makes you do bad things
• pressurized (¶9) — forced by constant asking

Summary cloze preview (Q8)

Q8 summarizes ¶4–¶7: trust → talk → don't push → reassure. Keep this order in mind.

October 26 · Am I a 'helicopter' or a 'snowplough' parent? — posted at 19.48 by MimiWong67

¶1

DE2 · Letter to the Editor · by Chris Wong

Dear Editor,

¶1
I am writing in response to your recent article 'Teen seeks counselling as parents work 50 hours a week'. It recounted the story of Toby Wan, a 14-year-old from Tai Po who had become increasingly depressed and isolated over the past year, spending hours on end in his bedroom playing video games and browsing websites. His parents only became aware of the severity of the problem when the school counsellor informed them that he had been having regular meetings with Toby.
¶2
Our initial reaction might be to think, 'How on earth did the parents not notice anything?' In reality, however, his parents might have been too busy and stressed themselves to see the warning signs. The materialistic culture of Hong Kong encourages us always to strive to better ourselves. Parents are under immense pressure to earn more and more money so that they can live in a bigger flat in a nicer neighbourhood, spoil their children with designer clothes, electronic gadgets and phones, and afford to take them on family holidays overseas.
¶3
In many families, both parents work full-time and their busy working days often stretch into the evening, meaning it is not uncommon for teenagers to be left unsupervised for hours on end. An older sibling or a domestic helper may step in to take over some aspects of the parental role, but in all honesty, this is not really their responsibility, and they will never be able to truly replace a parent.
¶4
The impact of this can be devastating for teenagers. Unsupervised teens can get caught up in online activities and spend too much time on the Internet chatting, gossiping, playing games and indulging in risk-taking behaviour. Even if parents have set screen-time limits for their children, if they are not at home to enforce them, it is not surprising that young people break the rules. Too much screen time can lead to difficulty sleeping and concentrating, poor academic results and even Internet addiction.
¶5
Furthermore, adolescence is a vulnerable stage in a person's life. What young people need the most at this time in their lives is guidance and unconditional love from parents. To fill the void, they may look for a support network online, where at best they may find unsuitable advice and at worst they may encounter shady characters who do not have their best interests at heart.

Dear Editor (cont.)

¶6
I believe employers have a role to play in tackling this issue. What we need is a radical shift in Hong Kong's working culture. Why not introduce legislation that caps the number of working hours in a week at 44? Companies could also instruct their employees not to check or reply to emails out of office hours. Parents also need to consider their role in contributing to teen stress. Two parents who work full-time may well be overachievers themselves. They expect their children to follow in their footsteps and will not tolerate failure. They may enrol their child in extra classes or pay for a private tutor, which means the pressure to succeed becomes even more intense for the student. A vicious circle starts to build as parents feel a commitment to earn enough money to pay for all of this. Stressed parents create a toxic atmosphere in the home that teenagers will most likely pick up on. Some parents may even lash out—verbally or physically—at teenagers who test their paper-thin patience. With both parent and child feeling stressed, it is little wonder that emotional bonds can be sorely tested during the teenage years.
¶8
There have also been some interesting studies into how the type of praise parents give can affect a child's performance, even at a very young age. If a child is consistently told 'You are smart,' this can lead to performance pressure: the child always feels that they are being judged on an outcome, rather than on the effort applied. Amazingly, one study found that three- and five-year-olds who were regularly told they were smart cheated more than those who were told they had performed well. Such pressure to excel is ingrained in Hong Kong society, and it seems that telling a child he or she is smart could encourage them to cheat in order to meet parental expectations. So what should parents do instead? Experts recommend giving praise for the effort ('You worked really hard on this painting.') and making it specific ('You did a good job on your maths homework.').
¶9
As a society, we need to re-examine the burden we place on our young people, and make sure that they are happy, rather than successful.

Write like a pro · Metaphor

A metaphor is a word or phrase used to describe something in a way that is different from its normal use and is not literally true. It shows that two different things share the same quality.

📖 From Text 2 (p. 57)

"…parents who either hover over everything their child does or who push all obstacles out of their kid's way. These overprotective parents don't want anything in life to be difficult or risky for their darling little cherubs. So, I stopped to ask myself, 'Am I a helicopter or a snowplough?'"

A person cannot literally be a helicopter or a snowplough — the metaphors show parents share the machines' qualities: hovering (helicopter) or pushing obstacles away (snowplough).

Why use metaphors?

• Make descriptions more powerful — The opening shots have been fired in the war against the virus.
• Say a lot in few words — Beyoncé is a goddess! (= strong, loved and admired by many)

🔑 Metaphor 常见于诗歌文学，但也可用于其他文体增强表现力。
Write like a pro · Simile

A simile is similar to a metaphor — but while a metaphor says something is something else, a simile uses like or as … as to compare two different things.

📖 From Text 2 (p. 57)

"I feel more like a big, fast-moving train, with no brakes, rattling down the tracks out of control."

The writer compares her parenting style to a runaway train using like — busy, fast and out of control.

Why use similes? (more common than metaphors — usable in most writing)

• Colourful descriptions — Her smile is as radiant as the sun.
• Easier to understand — Losing my best friend was like having my arm cut off.
• Humour — His singing was as musical as fingernails on a chalkboard.

Nouns · Countable and Uncountable

Nouns can be countable (Brian loves cats.) or uncountable (They love music.).

⚠️ Caution — collective nouns do NOT refer to an individual

❌ He is a staff of our company. → ✅ He is a member of staff / a staff member.
❌ She is a ground crew of the airline. → ✅ She is a member of the airline's ground crew.
❌ There were some police at the scene. → ✅ There were several police officers at the scene.

🇨🇳 Countable in Chinese, but UNCOUNTABLE in English

advice · baggage · bread · evidence · furniture · luggage · news · scenery · work (= job)

My brother is looking for work. (Compare: …looking for a job.) · Sichuan has spectacular scenery (❌ sceneries). (Compare: The Peak offers a beautiful view.)

🎧 Listening focus

In listening tasks, note whether nouns are countable or uncountable to avoid spelling and grammar mistakes.

Quantifiers for uncountable nouns

We cannot use numbers with uncountable nouns, but we can use phrases like:

a piece/pieces of (furniture) a jar of (honey) a glass of (water)
a box/several boxes of (chocolate) a drop of (oil) a carton of (milk)
a period/moment of (calm) a bar of (soap) a pound of (beef)
a lump of / a spoonful of (sugar) a game of (tennis) a loaf/slice of (bread)

☕ Drinks exception

Drinks like coffee, tea, beer, juice are uncountable — BUT we can say a coffee, two teas when we mean a cup/glass of the drink:
"I'd like two coffees and an orange juice, please," he said to the waiter.

Determiners · articles

We use determiners at the beginning of noun phrases to identify things or say how much/many we are talking about.

🔹 Indefinite article a/an

• first mention — I have a friend who lives in Vietnam.
• people's jobs — She is an English teacher.
• expressions of quantity — more than a thousand friends
• instead of per (price/speed/time) — HK$400 an hour

🔸 Definite article the

• already known/mentioned — The book I ordered still hasn't arrived.
• unique things — The moon is unusually bright tonight.
• some geographical names — The Great Lakes, the Atlantic Ocean
• superlatives & specific adjectives — The best student … the only one
• ordinal numbers — the third film
• groups of people — The Japanese have respect for the elderly.
• musical instruments — plays the violin

⚡ In some situations we use NO article — the zero article.

§2
Overprotective parents set lots of rules and restrictions that may prevent their children from having the freedom they want—freedom to meet friends, take part in activities and be a regular teenager. Behaviour like this can leave a young person feeling isolated, and can have a very negative impact on their self-esteem.
§3
What can you do if you have overprotective parents? Here are some helpful ideas.
§4 Start by following the rules
Even though you don't agree with them all, you need to show that you can follow your parents' rules. This will help them trust you.
§5 Talk to your parents
Your parents are protective because they love you and want to keep you safe and happy. Try to talk to them about how the restrictions on your behaviour make you feel, and why you want to have more freedom.
§6 Don't push too far too quickly
You shouldn't try to break any of the big rules, and don't try to persuade your parents to let you do anything too radical. If they don't want you to go out in the evening, don't stay out till midnight the first time they let you go.
§7 Reassure them with things that they want
If your parents ask you to send a text message when you arrive at your destination, make sure you do it, and why not send an extra one to say thank you for letting you go out?
§8 Build trust between your parents and your friends
Many parents think that their children's friends are not good for them and are a bad influence. Invite some of your friends over (one or two at a time), so your parents can meet them and learn to like and trust them.
§9 Be patient, keep trying
Your parents have been protecting you for a long time, and they have been setting rules for you for a long time too, so they may not accept changes very quickly. They also won't want to feel pressurized by you constantly asking them. You need to wait, take your time, be patient and try again until they start to give you some freedom.
§10 Be determined, put your foot down
Eventually, you may have to confront your parents. If this happens, it is important that you talk about how their rules make you feel, and how now that you are older, you really need more freedom. You don't want to make them feel guilty, but you do want them to be motivated to make changes.

DE2: Letter to the Editor by Chris Wong (Full)

§1
I am writing in response to your recent article 'Teen seeks counselling as parents work 50 hours a week'. It recounted the story of Toby Wan, a 14-year-old from Tai Po who had become increasingly depressed and isolated over the past year, spending hours on end in his bedroom playing video games and browsing websites. His parents only became aware of the severity of the problem when the school counsellor informed them that he had been having regular meetings with Toby.
§2
Our initial reaction might be to think, 'How on earth did the parents not notice anything?' In reality, however, his parents might have been too busy and stressed themselves to see the warning signs. The materialistic culture of Hong Kong encourages us always to strive to better ourselves. Parents are under immense pressure to earn more and more money so that they can live in a bigger flat in a nicer neighbourhood, spoil their children with designer clothes, electronic gadgets and phones, and afford to take them on family holidays overseas.
§3
In many families, both parents work full-time and their busy working days often stretch into the evening, meaning it is not uncommon for teenagers to be left unsupervised for hours on end. An older sibling or a domestic helper may step in to take over some aspects of the parental role, but in all honesty, this is not really their responsibility, and they will never be able to truly replace a parent.
§4
The impact of this can be devastating for teenagers. Unsupervised teens can get caught up in online activities and spend too much time on the Internet chatting, gossiping, playing games and indulging in risk-taking behaviour. Even if parents have set screen-time limits for their children, if they are not at home to enforce them, it is not surprising that young people break the rules. Too much screen time can lead to difficulty sleeping and concentrating, poor academic results and even Internet addiction.
§5
Furthermore, adolescence is a vulnerable stage in a person's life. What young people need the most at this time in their lives is guidance and unconditional love from parents. To fill the void, they may look for a support network online, where at best they may find unsuitable advice and at worst they may encounter shady characters who do not have their best interests at heart.
§6
I believe employers have a role to play in tackling this issue. What we need is a radical shift in Hong Kong's working culture. Why not introduce legislation that caps the number of working hours in a week at 44? Companies could also instruct their employees not to check or reply to emails out of office hours. In addition, companies could agree to flexible working arrangements for their staff, so that parents do not have to miss a child's music recital or football match. It is this kind of parent–child interaction that makes a child feel supported and loved.
§7
Parents also need to consider their role in contributing to teen stress. Two parents who work full-time may well be overachievers themselves. They expect their children to follow in their footsteps and will not tolerate failure. They may enrol their child in extra classes or pay for a private tutor, which means the pressure to succeed becomes even more intense for the student. A vicious circle starts to build as parents feel a commitment to earn enough money to pay for all of this. Stressed parents create a toxic atmosphere in the home that teenagers will most likely pick up on. Some parents may even lash out—verbally or physically—at teenagers who test their paper-thin patience. With both parent and child feeling stressed, it is little wonder that emotional bonds can be sorely tested during the teenage years.
§8
There have also been some interesting studies into how the type of praise parents give can affect a child's performance, even at a very young age. If a child is consistently told 'You are smart,' this can lead to performance pressure: the child always feels that they are being judged on an outcome, rather than on the effort applied. Amazingly, one study found that three- and five-year-olds who were regularly told they were smart cheated more than those who were told they had performed well. Such pressure to excel is ingrained in Hong Kong society, and it seems that telling a child he or she is smart could encourage them to cheat in order to meet parental expectations. So what should parents do instead? Experts recommend giving praise for the effort ('You worked really hard on this painting.') and making it specific ('You did a good job on your maths homework.').
§9
As a society, we need to re-examine the burden we place on our young people, and make sure that they are happy, rather than successful.
