📖 Full Article: The value of a plant

¶1
The Shenzhen Nongke Orchid is a delicate flower. Its centre is white, surrounded by a rich brush of magenta paint. These inner petals seem to grasp a small white and pink pearl. Fanning out from there are vibrant green petals, seeming to hold the centre safe from the outside world. It is a beautiful sight, and one enjoyed by very few. The Shenzhen Nongke Orchid sold at auction in 2005 for a startling 2 million Hong Kong dollars.
¶2
The anonymous buyers must have felt incredibly drawn to this scarce flower. Not only did it cost them a fortune, this flower only blooms every 4-5 years. Its blooms must feel like a celebration, a reward for dedication and patience. But there are other flowers whose beauty equals that of the orchid. What makes it worth such an incredible amount? It turns out that the Shenzhen Nongke Orchid has an unusual origin. The flower is completely human-made. Agricultural scientists at the Shenzhen Nongke Group developed the flower over a period of eight years. The company is known for its work manipulating genes in order to produce useful results for agriculture. In this case, though, the results were less useful than they were frivolous.
¶3
Humans do not need flowers to survive. Well, we do need pollinators like bees and beetles to go about their important work, producing fruits and vegetables for our diets, but the actual beauty of flowers has no bearing on our lives. Why then, are we so drawn to blooms for which people would be willing to spend as much as they would on a house or several luxury cars?
¶4
The same question could of course be asked about art. Throughout history, people have spent their fortunes on paintings and sculptures, but at least in those cases, they could not be destroyed by simply forgetting to water them. Neither can plants be passed down through families, like art often is. Most orchids survive for several decades, and that's with proper care. Flowers are both sensitive and fleeting. They do not last forever. But perhaps that's the point. For the very wealthy, do these super rare flowers serve as the perfect reminder that nothing lasts forever?
¶5
Another possibility is that the Shenzhen Nongke Orchid acts as a status symbol. Amongst wealthy circles, it is common to have expensive items that show belonging in the class of the elite. An orchid like this might be shown off at a dinner party, amazing guests while also signalling a certain status.
¶6
Without interviewing the buyers, it is impossible to know the exact motivation. But the urge to buy beautiful and expensive things is certainly not unique to the wealthy. The majority of people on earth feel the drive to own fashionable clothing, the latest gadgets, jewellery, designer pets, stylish furniture ... the list goes on and on and on. To witness our love of beautiful and useless things, we need not look any further than the diamond industry. Diamonds only really became popular starting in the 1930s. They became a must-have item, the ultimate symbol of long-lasting love. The idea that diamonds are rare is a more or less a fabrication. Somewhere around 120 million carats of diamonds are mined every year. But since we believe that diamonds are special and beautiful, we're willing to spend a large portion of our savings on them. It is our belief in these objects that creates their monetary value.
¶7
Though, diamonds, like art, can be kept for generations. One has to wonder how plants have managed to reach a similar price point. It's not just the Shenzhen Nongke Orchid. The popularity of rare plants has been increasing in the last several years. Instagram may be partly to blame for this trend. A variegated Monstera, a large unruly plant with striking white-patched leaves, sells for an amount close to most people's annual salary. Photos of these have proliferated on the social media site. Even a small cutting may sell for thousands of dollars. Depending on their age, bonsai trees can sell for hundreds of thousands, or even millions, of dollars. In 1981, a juniper bonsai sold for 15.6 million Hong Kong dollars at an auction in Japan. Plant collectors seem willing to part with an unfathomable amount of money to own rare and beautiful specimens.
¶8
The history of indoor plants stretches almost as far back as modern human history. Ancient civilizations in China, Greece, India and Egypt were known for cultivating plants indoors. It seems that the draw to have greenery indoors has been around for a very long time. As global exploration increased during the Renaissance, plants from different locales began travelling around the world. Imagine, at that time, having a plant that came from thousands of kilometres away. What better way to show off your worldly experience? Then, as people began to have furnaces in their homes, the variety of plants they could grow expanded. Now, it's a common sight to see plants in nearly every home you walk into. They've become as much a part of home decor as lamps or sofas. Perhaps it is only natural, then, that some people would want to highlight their style by choosing fancier foliage.
¶9
It should be said, too, that indoor plants are not completely without use. To say so is to overlook the benefits that they can bring to our lives. Multiple studies have shown that the presence of indoor plants can help us to feel relaxed. They provide us with oxygen. They make us feel as though we are outdoors, surrounded by nature. Caring for indoor plants, or shaping them, in the case of bonsai trees, provides us with a sense of purpose. But we can gain those benefits just as easily with a $10 spider plant as a $2 million dollar orchid.
¶10
Could the urge to spend hard-earned cash on indoor plants have something to do with the changing environment outside our windows? No one can deny, in this day and age, that nature is in peril because of climate change. Sometimes it feels like the world outside is spiralling outside of our control, with increasing droughts and other extreme weather. Maybe by bringing nature inside, we feel like we're able to control our own small slice of nature. In our homes, the flowers can bloom freely. They can have lush leaves even when the sky refuses to rain. We can live in a small forest even as forests are disappearing all around us. Whether we are wealthy or poor, having exposure to nature is something that affects every human on Earth.
¶11
It's a funny aspect of human nature that we think we could place a price tag on a flower. Or that we could create a flower with more value than what Mother Nature could produce. It is of course not us who owns nature, but nature who owns us. We would do well to remember that life is priceless.

Key Reading Skills · TFNG

📌 TFNG 解题框架

Step 1: 提取关键词 — 从题干中找名词、专有名词、数字等

Step 2: 扫读定位 — 用关键词在文章中找对应句，注意同义替换

Step 3: 精读比对 — 留意程度词(most/some)、绝对词(first/only/all)

Step 4: 三向判断

✅ True

原文提供足够证据支持题干，信息一致（含同义替换）

❌ False

原文有明确的相反或矛盾信息

⚠️ Not Given

原文完全未提及，无法判断对错

⚠️ False vs NG 的关键区别

False = 文中有证据推翻题干
NG = 文中根本没有提供足够信息作判断
"看起来合理" ≠ True，"找不到原句" ≠ False

Champions of Memory · ¶6–8

¶6
So how can we cultivate a super memory? Thankfully, many of the techniques used by memory athletes can be applied to our everyday lives. One way to remember more easily is to draw a picture of what you want to remember, either physical or mental. This requires you to think more deeply and in greater detail about the item. Think about any smells, tastes or emotions you associate with the item: the more detail and the more bizarre the picture, the more likely you'll be to remember it.
¶7
Then you'll need somewhere to store all these images: a memory palace. Think of a place you know well (your apartment, school, place of work), then imagine walking through it using a familiar route. Stick your images at different places along that route. When you want to remember, just walk back along the route and look at the images stuck to the walls. Interestingly, it seems that if you imagine yourself walking backwards, the technique is more effective than walking forwards. Experts speculate this is linked to our notion of the past stretching back behind us, and the future stretching ahead in front of us.
¶8
You can also use word association, for example to remember names. The first time you meet someone, conjure up a story to go with their name. Sandra Burley is a neurolinguist who has studied name recall and can always put a name to a face. 'Say for example I meet someone new, and his name is Robin Whitely. I think of the bird, a Robin, which has a red breast. So I think of Robin as a man who likes flying from one meeting to the next, flapping his arms instead of wings,' she explains. 'Then for the surname: I imagine Robin is depressed because the red colour of his breast has faded to white. So now he's flapping around everywhere, trying to find the red to put back in his breast. Robin Whitely. I won't forget his name any time soon.'

Text 4: The value of a plant — Full Passage

¶1
The Shenzhen Nongke Orchid is a delicate flower. Its centre is white, surrounded by a rich brush of magenta petals. These inner petals seem to grasp a small white and pink pearl. Fanning out from there, fan out in a gentle arc, seeming to hold the centre of the flower. It is a beautiful sight, one enjoyed by very few. The Shenzhen Nongke Orchid sold at auction in 2005 for a startling 2 million Hong Kong dollars.
¶2
The anonymous buyers must have felt inexplicably drawn to this scarce flower. Not only did it cost them a fortune, this flower only blooms every 4-5 years. Its blooms must feel like a celebration, a reward for dedication and patience. But there are other flowers whose beauty equals that of the orchid. What makes it worth such an incredible amount? It turns out that the Shenzhen Nongke Orchid has an unusual origin. The flower is completely man-made. Agricultural scientists at the Shenzhen Nongke Group in order to produce useful results for agriculture. In this case, however, the results were less useful than they were frivolous.
¶3
Humans do not need flowers to survive. Well, we do need pollinators like bees and beetles to go about their important work, producing fruits and vegetables for our diet, but the actual beauty of flowers has no bearing on our lives. Why, then, are we drawn to blooms for which people will be willing to spend as much as they would on a house or several luxury cars?
¶4
The same question could of course be asked about art. Throughout history, people have spent their fortunes on paintings and sculptures that can be passed down through families, could not be destroyed by simply forgetting to water them. Neither can plants wither in those cases. Plants like the orchid, however, survive for several decades. Bonsai plants can live for hundreds of years. A bonsai pine tree fetched 1.6 million Hong Kong dollars at an auction in Japan. Plant collectors seem willing to part with an unfathomable amount of money to care for and own beautiful specimens.
¶5
Another possibility is that the Shenzhen Nongke Orchid belongs to the status symbol type. Amongst wealthy circles, it is common to have expensive items that show you are in a certain class. An orchid like this might be shown off at a dinner party, a status symbol signalling a certain status.
¶6
Without interviewing the buyers, it is impossible to know the exact motivation. But the majority of people... to feel beautiful and expensive fashion is certainly not unique to the wealthy. Designer pets, designer furniture... But the drive to own fashionable things, the latest gadgets, designer jewellery, fashion... But people are born with the desire to own and show off, to want to own something that others do not have. It goes no further than that, and it is not unique. Diamonds are only beautiful and rare, because we are told they are rare. Any diamond is the ultimate symbol of long-lasting love. The popular idea that diamonds are not rare is a fabrication. Somewhere around 120 million carats of diamonds are mined every year. But since we believe that diamonds are special and beautiful, we are willing to spend a large portion of our savings on them. It is our belief in these objects that creates their monetary value.
¶7
Though, diamonds, like art, can be kept for generations. One has to wonder how plants have managed to reach a similar price point. The popularity of rare plants has been on the rise in the last several years. Photos of variegated Monstera, a large plant with striking white-patched leaves, have proliferated on social media. This plant's annual salary. Cuttings sell for thousands of dollars. Even a small cutting may sell for thousands of dollars. Depending on their bonsai age, bonsai trees can sell for hundreds of thousands, or even millions, of dollars. In 1981, a juniper bonsai sold for 15.6 million Hong Kong dollars at an auction in Japan. Plant collectors seem willing to part with an unfathomable amount of money to care for and own beautiful specimens.
¶8
The history of indoor plants stretches almost as far back as modern human history. Ancient civilizations in China, Greece, India and Egypt were known for cultivating plants indoors. It seems that the draw to have greenery indoors has been around for a very long time. As global exploration increased during the Renaissance, plants from different locales began travelling around the world. Imagine, at that time, having a plant that you had travelled thousands of kilometres to get. What better way to show off your worldly experience? Then, as people began to have furnaces in their homes, the variety of plants you could grow expanded. Now, it is common to see plants in nearly every home you walk into. They've become as much a part of home decor as lamps or sofas. Perhaps it is only natural, then, that some people would want to highlight their style by choosing fancier foliage.
¶9
It should be said, too, that indoor plants are not completely without use. To say so is to overlook the benefits that they can bring to our lives. Multiple studies have shown that indoor plants help us to feel relaxed. Caring for indoor plants, or shaping them, gives us a sense of purpose. Sitting by plants, surrounded by nature gives us a sense of calm. We can gain those benefits just as easily with a $10 spider plant as a $2 million dollar orchid.
¶10
Could our urge to spend hard-earned cash on indoor plants have something to do with the changing environment outside us? No one can deny, in this day and age, that nature is in peril because of climate change. Sometimes it feels like the world outside is spiraling out of control, with droughts and other extreme weather. Maybe by bringing nature inside, we feel like we have less control over our own small size of nature. We want to feel like we can control our own lives. When we are able to keep a plant alive, we feel like we are taming a small part of nature. Whether we buy a $2 spider plant or a $2 million orchid, the feeling is the same. It is a foolish or a wise pursuit? That is a small piece of nature we can call our own.
¶11
It is a foolish or a wise pursuit? No one can say for sure. But what we do know is that whoever could create a flower of more value than Mother Nature could produce it is not us who owns nature, but nature who owns us. We would do well to remember that life is priceless.
