💡 Reading skill 1: Positive & negative language Positive & negative language

When you read a text, pay attention to the language the writer uses. Is there a lot of positive language or a lot of negative language? This will help you understand the writer's opinions and attitude, as well as the tone of the writing.

To decide whether a text contains positive or negative language, pay particular attention to:
  • Adjectives: positive (beautiful, warm) or negative (ugly, cold)
  • Nouns: positive (advantage, neatness) or negative (disadvantage, mess)
  • Figurative language: positive (as bright as a button) or negative (as dull as ditchwater)
  • Other language: verbs (disappoint), adverbs of manner and degree (depressingly, exorbitantly), negatives (not inviting, didn't impress)

💡 Reading skill 2: Identifying tone and attitude Identifying tone and attitude

Some questions in the exam will ask you about the writer's tone or attitude. If you can identify whether the language in a text is positive or negative, this will give you an indication of the writer's tone. The tone shows the writer's attitude towards the subject.

A writer's tone may be positive, negative, serious, sarcastic, etc. His/her attitude may be ashamed, confident, confused, critical, enthusiastic, indifferent, positive, playful, negative, reflective, serious, supportive, sympathetic, worried, etc.
Tone

positive, negative, serious, sarcastic...

Attitude

ashamed, confident, confused, critical, enthusiastic, indifferent, playful, reflective, supportive, sympathetic, worried...

To identify the tone or the writer's attitude, think about:
  1. The writer's choice of words. Are they positive or negative?
  2. The details the writer includes. Does he/she use specific images or descriptions? What message do they send?
  3. The style of the writing. Is it very formal or informal?
  4. The punctuation the writer uses. Are there a lot of exclamation marks (!), dashes (—) and question marks (?)?

Text 1: My life as a restaurant intern（§1–§5 · Week 1）
§1
Well, I've made it through to the end of my first week as a kitchen intern — or stagiaire as I've learnt we're called! I'm working for Vegelicious, a chain of restaurants in Australia specializing in gluten-free and vegan food. They use only the freshest ingredients. I've tried some of the dishes and they're really mouth-watering! It almost makes me want to become a vegan ... almost! The internship lasts four months, and the first stage is in Sydney. After two months, I'll be moving to another restaurant in Perth.
§2
I have to say, I'm feeling a bit shocked right now. I'm not used to manual labour, so working in a hot, cramped kitchen for ten hours a day takes some getting used to. Last night, my legs were throbbing because I'd been on my feet for so long!
§3
The job itself is far from glamorous. There are lots of repetitive tasks to do, like washing salad leaves or peeling potatoes, which can be as dull as ditchwater. If you're thinking of getting into the restaurant business for excitement and an easy life, you can forget it!
§4
I've also struggled a little bit with the language barrier this week. I'm the only non-native speaker in the kitchen, and my co-workers speak fast and use slang. I can't always understand their accent. I'm worried I'm going to miss an important instruction! I feel a bit foolish when I have to ask someone to repeat what they've said for the second or third time. It also makes me feel a bit homesick — I've never been away from home before and I miss being able to chat effortlessly to my friends and family in Cantonese.
§5
To be honest, I'm not sure how I'm going to make it through the next four months. I'm counting down the hours until my internship ends — 830 to go!
Text 1: My life as a restaurant intern（§6–§10 · Week 17）
§6
Wow! I can't believe it's all over! My life as a stagiaire has come to an end. It's an experience I'll never forget.
§7
I'll admit that I struggled for about the first six weeks. I'm something of a perfectionist, and I was making a lot of mistakes. Of course I was — I didn't know anything at that stage! I kept preparing the wrong vegetables or misunderstanding my boss's instructions. Then one day, I put a load of apple peelings in the bin because I thought they were rubbish. It turned out one of the sous-chefs had been slaving over them for the past two hours for a special new dessert. He was rightly furious with me, and I burst into tears.
§8
I probably would have quit right there and then, but my boss took me aside and gave me some advice. ‘I still make mistakes,’ he said. ‘We all make mistakes. The important thing is to learn from them, and try not to make the same mistake again.’
§9
Then something extraordinary happened. It was as if a light bulb had gone on in my brain. I realized that in order to grow and learn, I had to step out of my comfort zone. After that little pep talk, I started to work hard to learn everything I could about how a professional kitchen works. I learnt cooking and baking techniques, I learnt about ingredients, and I learnt which dishes make the most profit. I threw myself into my work and I loved it. I got promoted, and on the last day the head chef presented me with a letter of recommendation. I couldn't have been more proud!
§10
Above all, my experience has taught me empathy for other people who work in the service industry. Next time I go out for dim sum or a pizza with my friends, I'll appreciate the work that has gone into preparing my meal. And I'll definitely leave a tip.
Text 2: How a culinary internship can be the recipe for success（§1–§5）
§1
You might think that an internship with a restaurant kitchen is only useful for those aiming to build a career in the catering industry, but you'd be wrong. The fast-paced, demanding world of the professional kitchen is a great place to learn many transferable skills that can serve you well no matter what industry you end up working in.
§2
The first rule of the kitchen is not to keep the customer waiting. According to Michel Sévres, an award-winning chef, professional kitchens are run at a breakneck speed. ‘It’s important our interns learn how to judge how long a task will take to complete, prioritize urgent tasks and make sure they meet deadlines.’
§3
If you’re a disorganized kind of person, a culinary internship will whip you into shape, and fast. ‘You’ll need to know where all your ingredients and tools are, and you’ll be continually cleaning up as you go along,’ explains Wendy Chung, who interned at a gourmet pizza restaurant last summer. ‘If you don’t, you’ll be working in chaos.’
§4
Also, don’t underestimate the social skills that this kind of work experience can nurture. Alice Kwah, HR manager for a Michelin-starred restaurant, explains: ‘You’ll need to be able to understand orders, ask for help when necessary (and offer help if you can), and take on board any criticism. You may well be working with people from around the world, which offers a unique chance to hone your language skills and become more open to foreign cultures.’
§5
At the end of your internship, whether you decide to pursue a career in catering or not, you'll have some valuable experience that looks great on your CV, and you'll be more employable. And if you manage to get a personal letter of recommendation from the head chef, the world is your oyster.

Text 3 (§1–§6)
§1
After I finished high school, I worked as a receptionist in a hotel for six months. It wasn't my dream job, but it enabled me to save up enough money to go travelling across India for three months.
§2
I'll never forget the shock I felt when I took my first hesitant steps onto the streets of New Delhi. The bustling city assaulted my senses from all sides: the raging heat that made my shirt stick to my back almost the second I stepped out of the air-conditioned hotel, the bright colours of traditional saris shimmering in the sunshine, and the constant noise and fumes from traffic that never stops and seems to obey no rules.
§3
I had chosen India because I wanted to go somewhere that would take me outside my comfort zone. My parents thought I was naive, but I wanted to experience a different cuisine, a different culture and a different language. India definitely didn't disappoint: it was like stepping onto an alien planet.
§4
In the second month, I was visiting a city called Jaipur. My tour guide, a man called Raj, showed me the Amber Fort and the Water Palace, and told me all about the city's history. Since we got on so well, he asked me if I would like to go to his home for some tea. I could not refuse my host's kind invitation. When I saw where Raj — who was so immaculately dressed and well groomed — lived, I was shocked. Somehow, nine people were squeezed into two tiny rooms, with only a single electric light bulb hanging precariously from the ceiling. The floor was made of dirt. And yet, Raj and his family were some of the happiest people I have ever met, and they were so welcoming that they treated me like one of the family.
§5
After meeting Raj and his family, I began to reassess my life. Did I really need so many possessions? Did I really need designer clothes and the latest phone to be happy? The answer was no, and yet I was looking forward to getting home to my comfortable flat with its air conditioning and big-screen TV.
§6
Travelling to India taught me something about myself that no textbook or school lesson ever could: I am incredibly blessed to live in a sophisticated and modern city like Hong Kong. My trip to India wasn't always easy, but it was worth it. It opened my mind and forced me to grow as a person. For that, and to Raj, I will always be grateful.

It seems fairly obvious that travel is good for us physically: whether we're strolling around a museum in a foreign capital or reading a good book on a sun lounger, a holiday offers us the chance to relax, de-stress from a hectic job or exams, and recharge our batteries. Increasingly though, experts are telling us that travel is also good for our mental well-being.
§2
One of the key benefits of taking a trip abroad is that it takes us away from our everyday lives and allows us to focus on different things. ‘We are so busy in our daily lives—working, paying the bills, doing chores, looking after family members—that we don’t have time to stop and think about what is really meaningful to us,’ says Dr Barbara McCary, a psychologist. ‘A holiday or—even better—an extended trip abroad gives us the chance to hit the "pause" button and think about what we want out of life and prioritize what really makes us happy.’ Dr McCary says she has known clients who make dramatic career changes or even move to a different country after such overseas trips. ‘And not one of them regretted it.’
§3
Travel also offers the opportunity to reinvent yourself, especially if you are prepared to go solo. In a foreign place with no friends or family around you, no one knows who you are, what your job is, where you come from or your history. You start with a clean slate. Liam Thomas founded a successful travel company catering to solo travellers, Room for One, after a life-changing holiday alone.
§4
Liam had a terrible motorbike accident just after he graduated from university, sustaining injuries to his lower legs. He was in hospital for six months and the rehabilitation process was long and hard. During that time, he lost a lot of friends and his world shrunk as he struggled to manage simple tasks he had once taken for granted. ‘It probably sounds ridiculous, but the day I could put on my socks by myself, I was so happy’ he says with a grin. ‘When I left the hospital, my confidence was at an all-time low,’ he explains. ‘My life was bleak and I knew I needed to do something dramatic to try to get my life back on track.’
Diagnostic article（§5–§8）
§5
As soon as he was able, Liam decided to travel around South America on his own. He was gone for just six weeks. ‘Suddenly, I was in exotic surroundings, there was a language barrier and everything seemed alien to me. I was a bit hesitant, but I had to interact with the world around me, however confusing it seemed, and this gave me a new-found sense of wonder. It was like I was seeing things for the first time. My trip was relatively short, but it made me re-evaluate who I was as a person and what I believed in.’ When Liam met new people at the first hostel he stayed at in Buenos Aires, he told them he was a student taking a gap year. ‘They didn’t know anything about my motorbike accident, so to them I wasn’t "poor Liam" who had spent half a year in hospital. I was a curious twenty-something who wanted to know more about another country and culture. It was like being reborn. Now I feel like the world is my oyster.’
§6
Going outside your comfort zone and planning a trip to a far-flung location or travelling on your own can seem a little bit frightening. When you actually do it, however, you might find that it is not as difficult as you expected. And even when things go wrong, travel can make you stronger emotionally, as you realize that you can handle situations that you might have shied away from at home.
§7
Amelia Wong travelled around Europe for eight weeks last summer, on her own. She had to cope with missed trains, hotels being fully booked and having her passport stolen. ‘It made me grow up,’ she says. ‘When my passport was stolen, I panicked at first. But then I gave myself a pep talk and managed to calm down. I went to the consulate and told them everything as calmly and confidently as I could. It all worked out in the end. At the time it seemed like the end of the world, but getting a replacement passport was actually not that much of a hassle.’
§8
Francis Tam experienced every skier’s nightmare when, travelling at break-neck speed down the slopes of Niseko, Japan, he fell and badly injured his knee. ‘At the time it was a living hell,’ he recalls. ‘I had to call for help to be able to get to the medical centre, and I had an operation at the hospital in Japan. Then I had to rearrange my flight back home, because I couldn’t bend my knee at all and needed three seats on the plane to stretch my leg out on.’ Although the experience was awful, Francis says it has made him more resilient. ‘If I’m ever in trouble again when overseas, I’ll know what to do and it won’t faze me.’
Diagnostic article（§9–§11）
§9
Even after a trip has ended, it can still have a positive impact on our mood and mental health. Studies have found that when we are on holiday, stress levels fall and we feel calmer. When we get back to our everyday lives, we can try to tap into those feelings of calm we experienced while away from home.
§10
Joe Burrill, an expert in mindfulness, explains: ‘One of the most relaxing holidays I have ever been on was in Boracay in the Philippines,’ says Burrill. ‘Now, if I feel like I’m starting to get a bit anxious about something, I just stop what I’m doing for 30 seconds, close my eyes and imagine I’m lying on that soft golden sand in Boracay. I take some deep breaths and try to remember what it felt like to be so relaxed. It helps me enormously when I need to calm down.’
§11
If you were ever unsure, the evidence now seems as clear as day: travel is good for your mind, body and soul.

✏️ Writing skill · Cohesive devices Cohesive devices

We use cohesive devices to connect ideas between sentences, between paragraphs and throughout a piece of writing as a whole. When you write, it is important to use cohesive devices correctly so that your writing is logical and the reader can follow it.

Connectives are commonly used in reflective writing to describe:
  • Sequence in time: after, before, then, until, when, while
  • Reason: as, because, since
  • Purpose: in order to, so that, to
  • Result: therefore, as a result, consequently
  • Example: for example, such as, for instance
  • Other cohesive devices: repetition, references, paraphrasing, contrast, comparison, condition, summary

🎵 Writing rhetoric · Alliteration Alliteration

When we use the same letters or sounds at the start of words that are close together, it is called alliteration.

Example

different cuisine, different culture, different language, India definitely didn't disappoint

The repetition of the consonant sound /d/ creates a rhythm and adds emphasis.

Using alliteration can help you:
  • draw the reader's attention
  • create a particular mood
  • make descriptions more vivid
  • make your writing flow better
  • create catchy titles
🎭 Writing rhetoric · Personification Personification

When we give human qualities, feelings or characteristics to things or ideas, it is called personification. In other words, we use personification to describe non-human things as if they were human.

Example

The bustling city assaulted my senses from all sides

To assault someone means to attack someone violently. Clearly, this is not something that a city can do. The writer is using personification to creatively describe how the city made him or her feel.

Using personification can help you:
  • create images in the mind of the reader
  • show creativity in your writing
  • make your writing more lively and interesting

📐 Grammar · Conditionals Conditionals
Learning objectivesMaster zero, first, second, and third conditionals, as well as other conditional expressions and unreal situations.

A conditional sentence consists of an if-clause (condition) and a main clause (result). Different tenses are used depending on whether the situation is real or unreal, and whether it refers to the present, past, or future.

1. Zero conditional

Use: general truths, scientific facts, imperatives

Structure: if + present simple, main clause + present simple

  • If we eat contaminated food, we become ill.
  • If you heat water to 100°C, it boils.
  • If you don't feel well, see a doctor.
2. First conditional

Use: things which may be true in the present, or which may happen in the future

Structure: if + present simple, main clause + will + bare infinitive

  • If it rains tomorrow morning, we will cancel the picnic.
  • If you are hungry now, I will make you some pancakes.

Special uses

  • Main clause: can/may for permission, may/might/could for possibility, can/could for ability
  • If-clause: present perfect or present continuous can be used
  • If-clause: should for less certain possibility (can omit if, invert with should)
  • If-clause: would for polite requests
  • Main clause: advice/suggestion expressions: you'd better, you should, why don't you, would you like...
3. Second conditional

Use: something which is not true or not real in the present or unlikely in the future

Structure: if + past simple, main clause + would + bare infinitive

  • If I had a car, I would take you for a ride around town. (= I don't have a car)
  • If he knew, I would ask him. (= he doesn't know)

Special uses

  • If-clause: past continuous can be used
  • Main clause: might/could for possible results or abilities
  • Main clause: would be + -ing for what would be in progress now
  • Formal: always use were in if-clause; informal: was is acceptable; if I were you for advice
4. Third conditional

Use: something which did not happen in the past (often expresses regret)

Structure: if + past perfect, main clause + would + have + past participle

  • If we had gone out, we would have been caught in the rain. (= we didn't go out)
  • If I had invested in the company, I would have made a lot of money. (= I didn't invest)

Special uses

  • If-clause: past perfect continuous can be used
  • Main clause: might/could + have + past participle
  • Can omit if, put had at the beginning (formal, more common in writing)
