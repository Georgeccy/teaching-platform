Section 2B · Q5–Q7
Paragraphs 4–6: Proofreading & Vocabulary

📖 Text 1: Paragraphs 4–6

¶4
First proposed officially in then-Chief Executive Tung Chee-hwa's 1998 policy address, it came about as the result of a Hong Kong Tourist Association survey conducted two years earlier which found that overseas visitors were dissatisfied with the lack of cultural attractions in the city. None too surprisingly, this caused critics to feel that the project was motivated more by a desire for increased tourism revenue than by any real interest in nurturing and developing the local arts scene.
¶5
Nevertheless, the original idea of a single venue on a 5.5-hectare site rapidly ballooned into a much more ambitious 40-hectare arts hub, and in April 2001, an international competition was launched to find a suitable design for the development. Legendary British architect Lord Norman Foster—the man behind the HSBC Main Building, Chek Lap Kok Airport and the Millennium Bridge in London, among countless other iconic structures—won out with a flamboyant plan that included a gigantic, 30-storey-high canopy.
¶6
The design immediately received significant backlash, much of it centred on the extravagant cost of construction brought about by the canopy. Additionally, with all the venues and facilities in the district to be linked under this one roof—the largest on the planet—it was decided that a single developer would be given control over the project. This caused further public outcry, as people felt it would give the developer too much power, likely resulting in a primarily residential and commercial development rather than a genuinely cultural one.

Section 3A · Proofreading
Proofreading — 改错题

Key insight: Proofreading tests content accuracy, not grammar. You compare a summary against the original text to find words that change the original meaning.

六步法 Six-Step Framework

Step 1: Read the summary, underline keywords to locate the right paragraph in the text.

Step 2: Re-read each line, focus on content words (nouns, adjectives, verbs).

Step 3: Find the original text — read the sentence closely.

Step 4: Word-by-word comparison: antonyms, factual confusion, wrong actions, wrong modifiers.

Step 5: Write the correct replacement word (same part of speech).

Step 6: Substitute back into the summary to verify the meaning matches the original.

⚠ 每行最多一个错误；无误则填 ✓
Section 3B · Chronological Ordering
Chronological Ordering — 时序排列

Key insight: Events are rarely presented in chronological order. Authors often start with the present, then flash back. You must reconstruct the real timeline using time markers.

五步法 Five-Step Framework

Step 1: Read all events to understand what they refer to.

Step 2: Locate each event in the text by its keywords.

Step 3: Extract time information: explicit years, relative time words (before/after/later/eventually), and causal logic.

Step 4: Build a timeline on scratch paper using the given anchor as reference.

Step 5: Number from earliest to latest.

⚠ 文章叙述顺序 ≠ 时间先后顺序！
Section 3C · Figurative Language
Figurative Language — 比喻与习语

Key insight: The writer uses idioms, metaphors, and implied expressions rather than literal language. Don't translate word-for-word — ask: in this context, what does the author mean?

四步法 Four-Step Framework

Step 1: Identify the non-literal expression — does it sound odd if read literally?

Step 2: Read the surrounding context — what situation is being described?

Step 3: Replace with plain language — say what it means in simple words.

Step 4: Verify — substitute your explanation back into the sentence and check it makes sense.

Examples from this passage:

• 'back at the drawing board' (¶7) → needed a total redesign

• 'redeemed itself somewhat' (¶9) → reputation improved a little

• 'raised a lot of eyebrows' (¶9) → surprised and unhappy

• 'put us firmly on the cultural map' (¶12) → become a recognized arts destination

Text 1: Four-day working week at Booster

¶1
Two months ago, the Manchester-based fintech start-up Booster became the latest in a rapidly lengthening line of companies to announce the implementation of a four-day working week for employees. In a recent exclusive interview, CEO Jacqueline Dawsey described the scheme as 'an unequivocal success'.
¶2
'If anything, it has exceeded our expectations,' she explained. 'We went into this hoping not to see a drop in productivity, but our early analysis in fact shows an increase, by a not insignificant 12%. Add to that the reduction we've experienced in our overhead costs, especially in our electricity bills, and it should really become clear to more employers that a four-day working week is not only feasible, but even financially advantageous.'
¶3
Dawsey stressed, however, that the decision was primarily motivated by concerns over the employees' well-being rather than the company's bottom line.
¶4
Booster has extended the weekend to also include Fridays. Unlike some, however, it is not requiring employees to compensate for this by working longer days. 'The whole point of this policy is to give staff sufficient time to recharge… This is why we have also extended our weekend email ban to weekday evenings.'

The History of the Weekend

📖 Text 2: A Brief History of the Weekend

¶1
The concept of a two-day weekend is surprisingly recent. Before the 20th century, most workers laboured six days a week, often from dawn until dusk. The idea of a regular day of rest was largely a religious one, with Sunday being observed as the Sabbath in Christian countries.
¶2
The push for a shorter working week began during the Industrial Revolution, when factory owners started to realise that overworked employees were less productive. In the late 19th century, some progressive British factory owners began to offer their workers a half-day on Saturday, in addition to the traditional Sunday rest.
¶3
It was not until the 20th century that the full two-day weekend began to take hold. In 1926, Henry Ford famously decided to close his car factories on both Saturday and Sunday, a move that was initially met with scepticism. To everyone's surprise, productivity actually rose.
¶4
The Great Depression of the 1930s, however, temporarily halted the progress of the five-day working week. With millions out of work, the two-day weekend was seen by some as a luxury the country could not afford. Nevertheless, by the 1950s, the five-day week had become the standard across most industries.
¶5
Fast forward to the present day, and the idea of a four-day working week is gaining traction at a rate that has surprised even its most ardent supporters. In countries like Iceland, New Zealand and Japan, pilot programmes have been launched to test the viability of a shorter working week, and the results have been encouraging.
¶6
In conclusion, the evolution of the working week has been marked by a series of incremental changes, each driven by a combination of economic necessity, social progress, and a growing understanding of what makes workers happy and productive. Some have described this trend as one that has progressed by leaps and bounds in recent years.
¶7
While some remain sceptical about the feasibility of a four-day week, the evidence from trial programmes suggests that it may well become the norm for future generations. As with the two-day weekend before it, what once seemed impossible can quickly become taken for granted.
¶8
The historical trajectory is clear: from six-day labour to Sunday rest, from half-day Saturday to the full weekend, and now to the prospect of a three-day break. The pace of change has accelerated dramatically, growing by leaps and bounds as technology reshapes the way we work.

📖 Text 1: Full Passage — West Kowloon Cultural District

§1
The twelfth of November 2021 saw the public opening of Hong Kong's spanking new visual art museum, M+, the most high-profile by far of the facilities slated to be included in the harbourfront West Kowloon Cultural District development.
§2
Widely praised for its expansive collections and cutting-edge architecture—courtesy of Swiss firm Herzog & de Meuron—the museum regularly drew daily crowds of more than 10,000 eager art lovers in its first seven weeks of being open. Then, rather characteristically for the ill-fated arts hub, the fifth wave of the Covid pandemic struck and forced the museum to shut its doors for more than three months.
§3
This setback was only the latest in a long line of mishaps and controversies that have plagued the West Kowloon Cultural District project since even before its inception.
§4
First proposed officially in then-Chief Executive Tung Chee-hwa's 1998 policy address, it came about as the result of a Hong Kong Tourist Association survey conducted two years earlier which found that overseas visitors were dissatisfied with the lack of cultural attractions in the city. None too surprisingly, this caused critics to feel that the project was motivated more by a desire for increased tourism revenue than by any real interest in nurturing and developing the local arts scene.
§5
Nevertheless, the original idea of a single venue on a 5.5-hectare site rapidly ballooned into a much more ambitious 40-hectare arts hub, and in April 2001, an international competition was launched to find a suitable design for the development. Legendary British architect Lord Norman Foster—the man behind the HSBC Main Building, Chek Lap Kok Airport and the Millennium Bridge in London, among countless other iconic structures—won out with a flamboyant plan that included a gigantic, 30-storey-high canopy.
§6
The design immediately received significant backlash, much of it centred on the extravagant cost of construction brought about by the canopy. Additionally, with all the venues and facilities in the district to be linked under this one roof—the largest on the planet—it was decided that a single developer would be given control over the project. This caused further public outcry, as people felt it would give the developer too much power, likely resulting in a primarily residential and commercial development rather than a genuinely cultural one.
§7
Thus, in 2006, the much-criticised canopy design was scrapped, and two years later, the West Kowloon Cultural District Authority was established to manage the development of the arts hub, on a budget of HK$21.6 billion. In other words, ten years after it was first proposed, the project was back at the drawing board, with no construction whatsoever having taken place in the interim.
§8
In fact, it was not until 2012 that the authority finally broke ground on the first venue in the district: the West Kowloon Bamboo Theatre, a temporary structure which played host to a series of Cantonese Opera performances and cinema screenings before being unceremoniously demolished. Hong Kong's culture vultures then had to wait another seven years before the first permanent venue, the Xiqu Centre, was finally completed.
§9
While the arts hub now seems to have redeemed itself somewhat with the opening of M+, questions certainly remain about the viability of the project. 'My main concern is with the cost,' said Professor Lolo Wong. 'Even the original budget, of 20+ billion Hong Kong dollars, raised a lot of eyebrows in the city—and deservedly so. Now, estimates for the total cost of construction have hit HK$70 billion, and considering how much still remains to be done, who can say for sure what the actual outlay will be for the taxpayers in the end? As much as I love art, we have to be realistic in our expectations of what ordinary people are willing to spend on it.'
§10
Professor Wong also expressed concerns about visitor numbers at the various venues within the arts hub. 'It was made explicit right at the start that this development was aimed primarily at overseas tourists. Well, for reasons that should be obvious to all of us, we have recently seen a significant decrease in those numbers, and it seems highly unlikely to me that local visitors will be able to make up for this shortfall.'
§11
Art historian Dr Ephraim Kwan was among those who take a more optimistic view. 'Yes, a project of this scale will inevitably be costly, but by way of comparison, the third runway at Chek Lap Kok is budgeted to cost HK$141.5 billion. I'm sure I'm not the only one who feels that this simple fact makes the West Kowloon Cultural District a relative bargain, especially considering that it's likely to expand the local arts scene—something I consider to be of great value to Hong Kong. There are those who claim that our city is a "cultural desert", and that people here are completely uninterested in art and culture. While this is rather an offensive exaggeration, it is certainly the case that many ordinary Hongkongers don't have much exposure to art. Clearly, this new arts hub will rectify that situation.'
§12
Additionally, Dr Kwan has high hopes that, once completed and operational, the West Kowloon Cultural District will help establish Hong Kong as an international arts centre. 'The most important works of art in the world tend to be exhibited in the most important museums in the world. If Hong Kong can lay claim to such a facility—or better yet, more than one—then there's no question that it'll make international headlines, and, as a result, put us firmly on the cultural map. Once that happens, all the time, money and effort spent on this project will undoubtedly have been worth it.'
§13
Regardless of how we feel about the history of the West Kowloon Cultural District, and about how the development was handled, Hong Kong now has an arts hub which at the very least has the potential to rank among the very best in the world. We should all wish for its success, and hope that it manages to attract both local and overseas visitors for decades—if not centuries—to come.
