Skills 1 · Rhetorical Devices
What are rhetorical devices?

Rhetorical devices are stylistic choices a writer makes to make a text more interesting, persuasive, creative or effective. Recognising them helps you catch the writer's real meaning — a must for DSE Reading.

🍎 Simile 明喻

Compares two things of different categories using like or as.

Tracey smokes like a chimney.

🔄 Metaphor 暗喻

Compares by saying one thing is the other — no "like/as".

…the slippery slope of addiction.

🧍 Personification 拟人

Gives non-human things human qualities.

I could hear the pizza calling my name.

🙃 Irony 反讽

Words mean the opposite of their literal meaning.

…his cardboard palace (a poor shelter).

🔑 判断口诀：有 like/as → simile；说"A 是 B" → metaphor；物当人写 → personification；正话反说 → irony。
Skills 1 · Learn by examples
Tap a card — guess the device & the real meaning
"Tracey smokes like a chimney."
which device?
Simile — she smokes a lot, like a chimney blowing out smoke. (health warning)
"People taking banned weight-loss pills are as mad as hatters."
which device?
Simile (idiom 'as mad as a hatter' = crazy) — shows disapproval of banned pills.
"…the slippery slope of addiction, low self-esteem, isolation…"
which device?
Metaphor — 'slippery slope' = an action that quickly leads into trouble.
"I could hear the pizza calling my name."
which device?
Personification — pizza can't speak; it means the speaker really wants to eat it.
"The homeless man survived the snowstorm in his cardboard palace."
which device?
Irony — 'palace' says the opposite; emphasises how poor his living conditions are.
Strategy 💡 "as mad as a hatter" is an…
tap
Idiomatic expression — words whose meaning differs from the literal. Learning idioms helps you decode rhetorical devices.

Skills 3 · Evaluating opinions and attitudes
Two questions to ask while reading

👥 Are other people's views mentioned?

A writer may quote others to support or reject a claim. How those views are presented reveals the writer's attitude towards them.

🎯 Who is the target audience? What is the purpose?

Ask why the writer wrote it (to inform / agree / disagree) and for whom (teens, parents…). Then you can guess what the writer wants readers to think.

🧰 Attitude word bank

concerned · indifferent · shocked · sorry · objective · subjective · approving · frustrated · critical · persuasive · sympathetic · cautious · positive · negative · neutral

🔑 态度词选择看"措辞+证据"：有数据/事实 → objective；只有个人看法 → subjective； luckily/encouraging → approving； sadly/shockingly → concerned。

Skills 4 · Extended response: quoting evidence
How to answer "give evidence / quote" questions

📋 The question type

Requires you to give reasons or information from the text to support an answer. Follow the instruction to quote or closely paraphrase.

Often appears in text order — you get a rough idea where the answer sits. If not, scan the whole text.

🪜 Steps

① Find key words in the question
② Mind the question word (Why/What/How)
③ Quote completely from the text

⚠️ Never use prior knowledge as evidence — always from the text.

🔑 引号题四问四型：Q1 改述+直接引用；Q2 描述+文中证据（不需原句）；Q3 定位段落找全两点；Q4 正反论点扫描全文。
Skills 4 · Read the story
What harm can it do?
¶1
Tommy had looked at his watch at least twenty times since the seminar began. A young nutritionist talked about how avoiding sugary drinks, staying away from fried foods and eating more fresh fruit and vegetables made people more energetic. This all sounded so bland. Was there anything healthy that tasted as good as his favourite tuck-shop snacks — freshly fried chicken wings and piping hot curry fish balls?
¶2
'It's just food,' Tommy thought. 'What harm can it do?' The school tuck shop actually provides healthy choices like chicken salad and sandwiches, but Tommy decided not to switch.
¶3
At lunch Tommy dashed to the tuck shop. He weighed 150 pounds and his BMI showed he was overweight. He no longer enjoyed sport — he would pant, his chest would ache and his legs would get sore three minutes into a game.
¶4
Tommy's parents both worked long hours and were often not home until after dinner. Tommy didn't know how to cook — but how many 14-year-old boys do? That was why he had to live on cheap street food or ready meals from convenience stores. How many school children are in Tommy's situation?

🧭 Before you answer

Each question names its paragraph — scan there. Q4 is different: it asks for both sides, so scan the whole text.

Key vocab

bland = tasteless · tuck shop = school snack shop · BMI = body mass index · ready meals = pre-packaged food

Skills 5 · Figurative language questions
3-step strategy 🪜

Figurative questions test the underlying meaning of figures of speech. You may need to: identify the device / what it refers to, and explain its intended meaning. Common types: simile, metaphor, irony.

① Locate

Find the figure of speech (a word or phrase) in the text.

② Reflect

Use what you know of the word/phrase to work out possible meanings beyond the literal.

③ Determine

From the context, pin down its intended meaning.

🔑 比喻题不是问"字面意思"，而是问"作者想表达什么"。答案永远回到上下文找线索。

Challenge · C1 — scan for TWO ways to combat nerves
Performance pressure: coach turned his team around
¶1
Jim Henson has been a diving coach in the US for years. A diver himself, he won gold in the three-metre springboard at the 1998 NCAA Championships while studying at a state university.
¶2-3
Three years ago he coached Thomas Kuiper, then 13 — a fearless diver in practice with near-perfect form. But at his first national competition: 'My heart started racing and my vision went blurry. I freaked out.'
¶4-5
Kuiper's form was messy and he missed a technical component. Henson read up on performance anxiety — 'choking' — knowing competition-day jitters often hurt performance.
¶6
Technique 1 — positive habit forming: Kuiper repeated the same simple dive — climb, walk forward, dive without pausing — until it was muscle memory.
¶7
Technique 2 — visualization: divers mentally picture their successful personal-best dive, to focus and to control pre-dive anxiety.
¶8-9
The process was slow but worked. Two years after choking, Kuiper returned and landed a perfect dive — gold. Teammates Ann Jacqewicz and John Howard took bronze and silver.

Reading Assessment · Letter to the editor
Penny-pinching on health care causes real pain
¶1
'To show how deeply he cares,' the Financial Secretary announced there would be no fresh funding for the health care voucher scheme for the over-70s. I felt compelled to register my displeasure.
¶2
The scheme is starving on a mere HK$1,000 a year. An MRI scan and follow-up could cost HK$6,000 — far more than the annual allowance.
¶3
The government has been too quiet. It's time to turn up the volume: distribute leaflets and posters, take out adverts — so vulnerable elderly people don't miss out.
¶4
Advocates assume most over-70s can self-medicate, but they can't unless they're made of money. Maybe you enjoy queuing for treatment, but most people have better things to do.
¶5
Some elderly don't use up their allocation (good health or gone private). I suggest using this unused cash to offer a more realistic HK$2,000 a year — transfer funds from the satisfied to the needy.
¶6
We have a long tradition of respecting the elderly. Let's not short-change them for the sake of a meagre saving to the central budget.
¶7
As a doctor here, I'm proud of my colleagues — our health service is the envy of many countries. But shouldn't they be doing what they were trained for — improving and saving lives? — James (Jimmy) Tang, M.D., Kowloon Tong

📖 Genre: letter to the editor

A reader's letter to a newspaper, commenting on a previously covered issue — positive or negative. Here: negative (a complaint about underfunding).

Performance pressure: coach turned his team around (Full)

¶1
Jim Henson has been a diving coach in the United States for years. He has coached girls and boys, men and women, and high school athletes. When Kuiper eventually dived, his form was messy and he missed one of the technical components of his dive.
¶5-7
Henson started reading up on performance anxiety, sometimes known as 'choking'. One technique was positive habit forming: Kuiper repeated the same simple dive — climb on to the board, walk forward and dive without pausing — until it was muscle memory. Next came visualization: divers find a comfortable position and mentally visualize their dives. This technique helped divers envision their successful personal best dive, to focus and to control pre-dive anxiety.
¶8-9
The process was slow and repetitive, but eventually it worked. Two years after Kuiper first 'choked', he returned to that very same diving competition. He climbed on to the diving board, walked steadily forward and landed a perfect dive. He got a gold medal. His teammates, Ann Jacqewicz and John Howard, received bronze and silver, respectively.
