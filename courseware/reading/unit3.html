Lead-in · The Artist Who Wrapped the World

Visual Shock (视觉冲击)

These are not photoshopped; they were real events. Discuss with your partner:

Q1: What are the buildings/parks covered with?

💡
Guidance
Fabric (布料) — millions of square feet of silver/gold fabric. Christo's works are not "built" but "wrapped" — he wrapped the Reichstag, coastlines, and the Arc de Triomphe. Keywords: wrap, fabric, monumental scale.

Q2: What forms of art are they?

💡
Guidance
Environmental art / Installation art (环境艺术/装置艺术). Unlike gallery paintings, Christo's art exists in public spaces — everyone can see it for free. This is part of his philosophy: art should be free for everyone.

Q3: How much time and money do you think it took to create these massive artworks?

💡
Guidance
The Reichstag wrapping took 24 years of planning and lobbying, costing about $15 million. All funds came from Christo himself — selling preparatory drawings. He refused sponsors and tickets. "It took 24 years to plan and only lasted 14 days — was it worthwhile?"
Meet the Artist & His Philosophy

Christo & Jeanne-Claude

Who is he?

Christo (and his wife Jeanne-Claude) was one of the most famous environmental artists in the world. He was known for wrapping famous landmarks or landscapes in millions of square feet of fabric.

His Artistic Philosophy — THREE Key Features:

1. Massive & Costly: Projects took decades to plan and cost millions of dollars.

2. Transient (短暂的): Despite the huge cost, artworks were displayed for only about 14 days and then completely removed. They left no trace.

3. Absolute Freedom: He never accepted money from sponsors and never charged tickets. He believed art should not be owned or bought by anyone.

💡
Teaching Guidance
• "Why would an artist refuse money from sponsors?" → Once you accept money, you lose control. Freedom > Money.
• "What does it mean for art to leave no trace?" → Contrast traditional art (museum/collection/permanent) vs Christo's art (public/experience/memory).
• "Is 14 days enough?" → Does transience make art more precious? Or is it wasteful?
Value Clash & Discussion

价值观碰撞

Q1: If an artwork only lasts for 14 days and then disappears forever, do you think the millions of dollars spent on it were "worth it"? Why or why not?

💡
Guidance
正方: The value of art is not in permanent possession — it's in experience and memory. Christo's works are precious because they are fleeting.
反方: Millions of dollars could build schools or hospitals. Is spending on "disappearing" art justified?
Christo's philosophy: "Leaving no trace" = absolute freedom — freedom from being owned by anyone.

Q2: The artist believed that because art could not be bought, sold, or kept, it represented "true freedom". Do you agree that "ownership restricts freedom"?

💡
Guidance
Christo's logic: Not being sold ≠ not being valued = not being controlled by collectors = absolute freedom.
Contrast: Museum paintings (owned, controlled) vs Christo's wrapped works (free, anyone can see).
His art's greatest "value" is precisely its un-purchasable, un-ownable nature.

Q44–Q47 · Paragraphs 3–4

PASSAGE — Paras 3–4

Para 3
In 1956, he stowed away on a train and fled to Vienna. From there he moved to Paris, where he met his future wife, Jeanne-Claude Denat de Guillebon. Together, they moved to a loft in Manhattan, New York, and began a collaborative partnership that would last the next 50 years, until Jeanne-Claude's death in 2009 from a brain aneurysm. Christo was the artist. Jeanne-Claude the organizer. Their commitment to their art was absolute, to the point that whenever they travelled, they flew on separate planes so that in the event of a crash, one could complete the other's work.
Para 4
Such devotion to their craft was a common thread throughout their work, and meant that they pursued seemingly impossible objectives with what some would say was foolhardy doggedness. Some installations took decades of planning and bureaucratic wrangling: the Reichstag project was 24 years in the making. In most other cases, despite the many hours spent battling, the final outcome was defeat. For the 22 major works they completed, 37 more failed to get permission. The legal and political battles required often became part of the creative endeavour for Christo and Jeanne-Claude. They even divided their creative process into two distinct stages, the first phase involved drawing, sketching, planning and scouting locations, including getting the necessary permissions from authorities. This could take years and be hugely expensive (renting Park costs $3 million). The second phase involved the nuts and bolts — hiring workers, making mock-ups and ordering materials.

It should be acknowledged that charm had a role to play in the artists' success, too. Q52–Q56 · Paragraphs 8–9

PASSAGE — Paras 8–9

Para 8
A further attraction was that their works were easily accessible, and there was no entrance fee: Christo funded them himself by selling his sketches, models and collages through his own company, thereby bypassing the commission charged by galleries. (Such canny business acumen bankrolled Christo's career and made its most audacious works possible.) Before he had erected even one of the steel posts to support the 7,503 fabric gates in Central Park, he had already sold them on to the Chinese construction industry. The installations didn't belong to anyone and could never be owned. 'The reason we don't like the projects to stay is no one can charge for tickets, no one can buy this project,' Christo said in 2016. 'It is freedom. Freedom is the enemy of possession, and possession is permanence.' It could be argued that Christo's work also represented freedom of thought both in terms of the creator and the viewer. Each person's interpretation of Christo's art is as valid as the next.
Para 9
At its most basic, Christo and Jeanne-Claude's work challenges us to examine our perception of the environment around us. 'They wanted to enhance public spaces, to make them more beautiful, and make it possible for everyone to see that beauty,' says Axel Lens, an art critic. 'They dream big, so that we, as viewers, could be small — no more than a speck in the upgraded landscape.' In 1969, they wrapped the coastline of Australia — 100,000 square metres of fabric, taking 100 workers almost 20,000 hours. In 1991, they installed thousands of umbrellas in Tokyo, where people would come to picnic beneath. In 2016, they installed a series of walkways made of yellow fabric on Lake Iseo, Italy. The installation lasted only 16 days, but was Christo's most visited work.

Together, they moved to a loft in Manhattan, New York, and began a collaborative partnership that would last the next 50 years, until Jeanne-Claude's death in 2009 from a brain aneurysm. Christo was the artist. Jeanne-Claude the organizer. Their commitment to their art was absolute, to the point that whenever they travelled, they flew on separate planes so that in the event of a crash, one could complete the other's work.
Para 4
Such devotion to their craft was a common thread throughout their work, and meant that they pursued seemingly impossible objectives with what some would say was foolhardy doggedness. Some installations took decades of planning and bureaucratic wrangling: the Reichstag project was 24 years in the making. In most other cases, despite the many hours spent battling, the final outcome was defeat. For the 22 major works they completed, 37 more failed to get permission. The legal and political battles required often became part of the creative endeavour for Christo and Jeanne-Claude. They even divided their creative process into two distinct stages, the first phase involved drawing, sketching, planning and scouting locations, including getting the necessary permissions from authorities. This could take years and be hugely expensive (renting Park costs $3 million). The second phase involved the nuts and bolts — hiring workers, making mock-ups and ordering materials.
Para 5
It should be acknowledged that charm had a role to play in the artists' success, too. Early in their careers, Christo and Jeanne-Claude set up a blockade of oil barrels in Rue Visconti in Paris in protest against the Berlin Wall. The artists had been refused permission for the project. Instead, they sweet-talked police officers and landlords to let them create their art, and then to leave it there for the public to enjoy. Better to beg for forgiveness than ask for permission, as the saying goes. However, their motivity and ever-more ambitious plans eventually put paid to such impromptu creativity. As their fame grew, so did the size of the crowds they attracted.
Para 6
Why were people so drawn to Christo's unique public artworks? They were huge, and often pointless, but this somehow added to their appeal. 'How often in life would you get the chance to see the Reichstag wrapped in plastic? It was a sight not to be missed,' says Darren Mayhew, who is writing a biography of the couple. The transient nature of the work added to the allure.
Para 7
There was certainly something enticing about the artworks being on display for only a few weeks, despite having taken years to plan and millions to create, as if underscoring the fragility and fleeting nature of life. 'Our works are temporary in order to endow the works of art with the feeling of urgency to be seen, and the love and tenderness brought by the fact that they will not last,' the artists explained in 2005.
Para 8
A further attraction was that their works were easily accessible, and there was no entrance fee: Christo funded them himself by selling his sketches, models and collages through his own company, thereby bypassing the commission charged by galleries. (Such canny business acumen bankrolled Christo's career and made its most audacious works possible.) Before he had erected even one of the steel posts to support the 7,503 fabric gates in Central Park, he had already sold them on to the Chinese construction industry. The installations didn't belong to anyone and could never be owned. 'The reason we don't like the projects to stay is no one can charge for tickets, no one can buy this project,' Christo said in 2016. 'It is freedom. Freedom is the enemy of possession, and possession is permanence.' It could be argued that Christo's work also represented freedom of thought both in terms of the creator and the viewer. Each person's interpretation of Christo's art is as valid as the next.
Para 9
At its most basic, Christo and Jeanne-Claude's work challenges us to examine our perception of the environment around us. 'They wanted to enhance public spaces, to make them more beautiful, and make it possible for everyone to see that beauty,' says Axel Lens, an art critic. 'They dream big, so that we, as viewers, could be small — no more than a speck in the upgraded landscape.' In 1969, they wrapped the coastline of Australia — 100,000 square metres of fabric, taking 100 workers almost 20,000 hours. In 1991, they installed thousands of umbrellas in Tokyo, where people would come to picnic beneath. In 2016, they installed a series of walkways made of yellow fabric on Lake Iseo, Italy. The installation lasted only 16 days, but was Christo's most visited work.
Para 10
With so many projects taking decades to come to fruition, it is not surprising that Christo's work is not yet complete. An installation already well advanced in planning at the time of Christo's death, will go ahead without him. In 2021 the Arc de Triomphe in Paris to be wrapped according to the specifications. He won't be there to see it, but his legions of fans will honour his memory by making a pilgrimage to what will be perhaps his last work of public art.
Key Skills · Tone & Attitude (语气与作者立场)

读出"作者怎么说"

相同的事实，作者选用不同的词、不同的句式，就透露出不同的情绪与立场。处理这类题目，需从三个层面入手：

① 词语情感色彩

这个词本身带有什么情感或态度倾向？

② 缓冲语言

作者有没有用特定短语来"弱化"或"保留"立场？

③ 全文语气

综合全文，作者的整体情绪与态度是什么？

题型考点典型问法
Word Choice / Connotation识别具体用语背后的情感或态度倾向What is suggested when the text states that...?
Hedging Language识别作者表达不确定或保留态度的短语Which phrase shows the writer does not necessarily agree that...?
Writer's View从段落推断作者的真实立场并作出解释Does the writer think...? Explain your answer.
Tone MCQ判断全文的整体语气Which of the following best describes the writer's tone?

💡 Word Choice / Connotation 解题框架

Step 1：定位该词，读完整句——不能孤立看词，须连同前后句获取完整语境。
Step 2：问"换一个普通词会怎样"——把该词替换成意思相近但情感中性的词，两者差异就是额外传递的态度。
Step 3：判断作者立场倾向——正面、负面、讽刺、同情、钦佩？
⚠️ 词语含义不只是字典释义，还包括它在语境中传递的情感色彩。选词即立场。

💡 Hedging Language 解题框架

Step 1：找到题目指向的句子，定位含有该短语的完整句。
Step 2：问"没有这个短语，句子变成什么"——去掉 hedging 词后，句子变成绝对陈述，差距就是作者刻意保留的"距离感"。
Step 3：判断作者真实态度——引用别人观点？提出未经证实的理论？还是表达自己的不确定？

类型例子
引入他人观点some would say / it could be argued that / according to some
表达不确定perhaps / possibly / it seems / it appears
软化绝对叙述in many cases / to some extent / largely

💡 Writer's View (作者观点题) 解题框架

Step 1：找到题目指向的段落，精读——Writer's View 题通常指定某段落，答案在该段落内。
Step 2：找作者直接表态的句子——留意 surprisingly / It is clear that / fortunately / of course / it is worth noting / in my view 等信号词。
Step 3：引用依据，用自己的话解释——答案须包含两部分：立场 (Yes/No) + 文中依据 (引用或 paraphrase)。

💡 Tone MCQ 解题框架

Step 1：排除明显不符的选项——逐项对照文章整体，有没有哪个选项描述的情绪在文中完全找不到依据？
Step 2：寻找全文情感基调的证据——作者的用词选择、段落语气、对人物的描述方式。
Step 3：选最能覆盖全文的选项——Tone MCQ 考的是整篇文章的主导语气，而非某一段的情绪。

Key Skills · Views (人物观点)

识别和区分文章中不同人物的立场

当文章引用多个人的说法时，你需要读懂每段话背后的核心立场，并判断它属于谁。Q63 正是考查这种能力。

题型考点典型问法
Views Attribution将引言或观点与对应人物匹配Write the name of the person next to the statement. Do not use any name more than once.
Writer's View识别作者在文中表达的立场Does the writer think...? / What is the writer's attitude towards...?

💡 Views 解题框架

Step 1：阅读题目，明确涉及哪些人物——先在文中找到每个人物出现的段落，了解各自的基本立场。
Step 2：逐一分析每段引言的核心立场——不要只看字面，问：核心观点是什么？持这个观点的人是支持、质疑，还是中立？
Step 3：与人物立场对应——将引言的核心立场与文中各人物的已知立场比对，找到最吻合的匹配。
Step 4：注意 paraphrase——引言通常不是原文照搬，需识别同义替换后再匹配。
⚠️ 每个人物只能使用一次。当两段引言都像同一人时，需结合整体判断，选择"最匹配"而非"也能匹配"的。

Para 1
We are all well aware that we should never judge a book by its cover. But what if the 'book' is a human? Well, then, according to the Human Library Organization, a non-profit based in Copenhagen, Denmark, it is incumbent upon us to just reserve our judgement but also to do our very best to unjudge them.
Para 2
The idea behind the charity is ingenious yet simple — a common characteristic of truly genuine ideas — and goes back to the year 2000 and an international music festival near the Danish capital, where, on 29 June, the first human library opened for business on a small patch of grass. With the aim of challenging prejudices and stereotypes about minorities and people who differ from what is considered the norm, 50 different human 'books', including a Muslim, a parking enforcement officer and a resident of an anarchist commune, were 'published' for the edification of a group of curious 'readers'.
Para 3
Stripping away the metaphors for a moment, what this amounted to was that participants were given a chance to hear a member of a stigmatized group tell their story and present their views, and encouraged to ask any personal questions of them that they liked in return — as long as they did so with respect — in the hope that they would leave the encounter with a better understanding and new-found empathy.
Para 4
Since its inception, the Human Library has moved from presenting small selections of books at local events to offering up shelves upon proverbial shelves of titles all around the world through its branches in more than 80 countries. The range of the offerings has also expanded considerably, and now contains a variety of topics based on 12 'pillars of prejudice', including ethnicity, mental health, disabilities, social status, occupation and religion. The basic mission remains the same, however, to combat the othering of marginalized and stigmatized groups by demystifying them to the public through simple interaction and conversation.
Para 5
'All people judge and so we are not here to change your mind or to tell you not to judge,' explains Ronni Abergel, the journalist and human rights activist who founded the organization. Text 4: Christo — The Artist Who Wrapped the World (Full Passage)

§1
Christo Vladimirov Javacheff, the artist famous for wrapping the Berlin Reichstag in 1.1 million square feet of plastic and erecting more than 7,500 gates made from steel and covered with orange cloth in Central Park, New York, died at the age of 84. His artistic legacy is intangible: most of his artworks lasted only a matter of days. Yet they had a profound impact on those who saw them.
§2
Born in Bulgaria in 1935, Christo studied at the Sofia Academy of Fine Arts during the time of Soviet rule. Students were required to study the propaganda effort, and so Christo was sent to the peasants' fields to make sure they looked attractive and 'sent the right message' to tourists passing through in luxury 'on the Orient Express'. His own work would later come to represent the polar opposite of this enforced training.
§3
In 1956, he stowed away on a train and fled to Vienna. From there he moved to Paris, where he met his future wife, Jeanne-Claude Denat de Guillebon. Together, they moved to a loft in Manhattan, New York, and began a collaborative partnership that would last the next 50 years, until Jeanne-Claude's death in 2009 from a brain aneurysm. Christo was the artist. Jeanne-Claude the organizer. Their commitment to their art was absolute, to the point that whenever they travelled, they flew on separate planes so that in the event of a crash, one could complete the other's work.
§4
Such devotion to their craft was a common thread throughout their work, and meant that they pursued seemingly impossible objectives with what some would say was foolhardy doggedness. Some installations took decades of planning and bureaucratic wrangling: the Reichstag project was 24 years in the making. In most other cases, despite the many hours spent battling, the final outcome was defeat. For the 22 major works they completed, 37 more failed to get permission. The legal and political battles required often became part of the creative endeavour for Christo and Jeanne-Claude. They even divided their creative process into two distinct stages, the first phase involved drawing, sketching, planning and scouting locations, including getting the necessary permissions from authorities. This could take years and be hugely expensive (renting Park costs $3 million). The second phase involved the nuts and bolts — hiring workers, making mock-ups and ordering materials.
§5
It should be acknowledged that charm had a role to play in the artists' success, too. Early in their careers, Christo and Jeanne-Claude set up a blockade of oil barrels in Rue Visconti in Paris in protest against the Berlin Wall. The artists had been refused permission for the project. Instead, they sweet-talked police officers and landlords to let them create their art, and then to leave it there for the public to enjoy. Better to beg for forgiveness than ask for permission, as the saying goes. However, their motivity and ever-more ambitious plans eventually put paid to such impromptu creativity. As their fame grew, so did the size of the crowds they attracted.
§6
Why were people so drawn to Christo's unique public artworks? They were huge, and often pointless, but this somehow added to their appeal. 'How often in life would you get the chance to see the Reichstag wrapped in plastic? It was a sight not to be missed,' says Darren Mayhew, who is writing a biography of the couple. The transient nature of the work added to the allure.
§7
There was certainly something enticing about the artworks being on display for only a few weeks, despite having taken years to plan and millions to create, as if underscoring the fragility and fleeting nature of life. 'Our works are temporary in order to endow the works of art with the feeling of urgency to be seen, and the love and tenderness brought by the fact that they will not last,' the artists explained in 2005.
§8
A further attraction was that their works were easily accessible, and there was no entrance fee: Christo funded them himself by selling his sketches, models and collages through his own company, thereby bypassing the commission charged by galleries. (Such canny business acumen bankrolled Christo's career and made its most audacious works possible.) Before he had erected even one of the steel posts to support the 7,503 fabric gates in Central Park, he had already sold them on to the Chinese construction industry. The installations didn't belong to anyone and could never be owned. 'The reason we don't like the projects to stay is no one can charge for tickets, no one can buy this project,' Christo said in 2016. 'It is freedom. Freedom is the enemy of possession, and possession is permanence.' It could be argued that Christo's work also represented freedom of thought both in terms of the creator and the viewer. Each person's interpretation of Christo's art is as valid as the next.
§9
At its most basic, Christo and Jeanne-Claude's work challenges us to examine our perception of the environment around us. 'They wanted to enhance public spaces, to make them more beautiful, and make it possible for everyone to see that beauty,' says Axel Lens, an art critic. 'They dream big, so that we, as viewers, could be small — no more than a speck in the upgraded landscape.' In 1969, they wrapped the coastline of Australia — 100,000 square metres of fabric, taking 100 workers almost 20,000 hours. In 1991, they installed thousands of umbrellas in Tokyo, where people would come to picnic beneath. In 2016, they installed a series of walkways made of yellow fabric on Lake Iseo, Italy. The installation lasted only 16 days, but was Christo's most visited work.
§10
With so many projects taking decades to come to fruition, it is not surprising that Christo's work is not yet complete. An installation already well advanced in planning at the time of Christo's death, will go ahead without him. In 2021 the Arc de Triomphe in Paris to be wrapped according to the specifications. He won't be there to see it, but his legions of fans will honour his memory by making a pilgrimage to what will be perhaps his last work of public art.

The Human Library Organization (Full Passage)

§1
We are all well aware that we should never judge a book by its cover. But what if the 'book' is a human? Well, then, according to the Human Library Organization, a non-profit based in Copenhagen, Denmark, it is incumbent upon us to just reserve our judgement but also to do our very best to unjudge them.
§2
The idea behind the charity is ingenious yet simple — a common characteristic of truly genuine ideas — and goes back to the year 2000 and an international music festival near the Danish capital, where, on 29 June, the first human library opened for business on a small patch of grass. With the aim of challenging prejudices and stereotypes about minorities and people who differ from what is considered the norm, 50 different human 'books', including a Muslim, a parking enforcement officer and a resident of an anarchist commune, were 'published' for the edification of a group of curious 'readers'.
§3
Stripping away the metaphors for a moment, what this amounted to was that participants were given a chance to hear a member of a stigmatized group tell their story and present their views, and encouraged to ask any personal questions of them that they liked in return — as long as they did so with respect — in the hope that they would leave the encounter with a better understanding and new-found empathy.
§4
Since its inception, the Human Library has moved from presenting small selections of books at local events to offering up shelves upon proverbial shelves of titles all around the world through its branches in more than 80 countries. The range of the offerings has also expanded considerably, and now contains a variety of topics based on 12 'pillars of prejudice', including ethnicity, mental health, disabilities, social status, occupation and religion. The basic mission remains the same, however, to combat the othering of marginalized and stigmatized groups by demystifying them to the public through simple interaction and conversation.
§5
'All people judge and so we are not here to change your mind or to tell you not to judge,' explains Ronni Abergel, the journalist and human rights activist who founded the organization. 'We are here to better inform available to you in a safe setting so you can make your own decisions, but hopefully make informed decisions based not on quick judgement but after more careful consideration and after meeting someone that knows about it. [This] gives you a chance to "unjudge" someone.'
§6
Over the years, the Human Library has facilitated countless interactions between people from all walks of life, whether in a group format or on a person-to-person basis, resulting in such unlikely pairings as a feminist meeting with a Muslim woman in a hijab asking if she wears it by choice or confusion, a Black anti-racist activist meeting with a supporter of former US President Donald Trump, and a transgender woman meeting with a conservative woman who believes women living in sin. More often than not, such encounters end with both parties having benefitted and become more empathetic from the experience.
§7
The human books are all volunteers who obviously have their own personal motivations for inviting complete strangers to flick through their pages. Often, though, it comes down to a desire to encourage these strangers not to judge others based on first impressions and their own innate biases. Such was the case for Nahian Bushra, a visually impaired woman from Bangladesh, who joined the Dhaka Human Library for the age of 20, after having noticed this tendency in people around her.
§8
'When they look at me, all they probably see is a visually impaired girl,' Bushra explains, 'but they have no idea about my strength, they have no idea about how many hurdles I have overcome to be the person I am today. I wanted to be the beam of hope for the people who are losing to darkness and, therefore, I chose to become a human book.'
§9
The one thing the Human Library doesn't tolerate in its volunteers is an overt agenda. Abergel stresses that those who apply just to engage in political debates, or to force their opinions on others, are unequivocally rejected. 'We're completely neutral, or neutral as Switzerland,' he told CNN in an interview. 'As long as you're not preaching hate or intolerance, you have potential to our bookshelf.'
§10
The non-partisan nature of the organization has made it appealing not only to individual readers looking for a safe space to better themselves, but also to companies who are serious about diversity and inclusion in the workplace and committed to corporate social responsibility. This has seen the NGO hosting reading events for corporate grants such as Microsoft, Heineken and IKEA, and even helping some of them create internal human bookshelves. These efforts have been very well received by participants and proved highly enlightening.
§11
'I could really feel when my prejudice bubbled up and confronting that was insightful,' said a StubHub employee following a reading event. A representative of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) echoed these sentiments, exclaiming, 'This should be a mandatory event for all staff!'
§12
The Human Library's approach to D&I also has applications for medical schools, several of which have hosted reading events and developed programmes in partnership with the organization over the last couple of years — also to great success. One such institution is the University of Glasgow's School of Medicine, which in 2022 became the first medical school in the UK to introduce such a programme. Dr Lynsay Crawford, Senior Clinical University Lecturer, outlined the reasoning behind a partnership:
§13
'Medical students need to have a wide knowledge base that can be learnt from traditional books, but to be truly effective and compassionate doctors they need to develop more nuanced skills — communication, empathy, listening, reflection — and what better way to achieve that than through interactions and connections with people and their lived experience — the human books.'
§14
While praise for the Human Library has been near universal in the media, some detractors have pointed out that the people who are willing to participate in its readings are already open-minded by definition, making such events a case of preaching to the converted. And yes, indeed, as Human Library signs up. The same cannot be said for corporate or academic participants, however, who do not, as a rule, sign up of their own volition. These people have reported very similar benefits to those who were more predisposed to the NGO's approach, including leaving the events with some of their prejudices in tatters and a better understanding of the experiences of their fellow humans.
§15
It would seem to follow, then, the more people who take part in such events — whether voluntarily or through their workplace or school, and whether organized by the Human Library or another organization — the more empathetic we'll become as a species. And who could possibly make the argument that that's anything but a good thing?
