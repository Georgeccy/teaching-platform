📖 概念总结
Positive ↔ Negative：反义词配对
👀 下面交替出现正面/负面词，先想想它们的反义词，再点按提示查看
1. ✅ amazing (惊人的) → 反义词: terrible, awful, horrible
2. ❌ disappointing (令人失望的) → 反义词: satisfying, encouraging, uplifting
3. ✅ comfortable (舒适的) → 反义词: uncomfortable, cramped, claustrophobic
4. ❌ rude (粗鲁的) → 反义词: polite, friendly, welcoming
5. ✅ spacious (宽敞的) → 反义词: cramped, overcrowded, claustrophobic
6. ❌ overpriced (过贵的) → 反义词: affordable, reasonable, good value
7. ✅ recommend (推荐) → 反义词: avoid, warn against, steer clear of
8. ❌ forgettable (易忘的) → 反义词: unforgettable, memorable, lasting

📌 小结：Positive/Negative vocabulary 就像一对对反义词 — DSE 阅读里你要快速识别它们！

Santorini's: The Worst Pizza! ★
Textbook p.174, A1 — Customer Review

What can I say about Santorini's, except avoid this place! When we arrived, our hostess was not at all welcoming; in fact I'd say she was rude. She didn't make eye contact with us, and she didn't smile even once! We had a long wait for our food to arrive. When it did, the pizzas were cold and tasteless. We finished our meals as quickly as we could and reluctantly paid the extortionate bill of €175. If you're thinking of trying Santorini's, don't! It's an overrated tourist trap.

🧑‍🏫 老师带练
Level 1: Tower Bridge Hotel 🏨
Textbook p.175, A2 — Ruby Wong's review

I stayed here at the end of August and what a disappointment! First, I had to wait more than 15 minutes to check in. I had booked online and paid extra for a room with a view of Tower Bridge, but in fact my room looked out at an ugly alley full of rubbish! It was depressing! The room itself was very small. It made me feel claustrophobic and it was not very well sound-proofed. This meant that when staff began cleaning at 6 a.m., it woke me up. When I'm on holiday, I want to relax and have a nice lie-in, not get woken up at the crack of dawn!

🧑‍🏫 老师带练
Level 3: Mayan Dreams Resort 🌴
Textbook p.175, A2 — Ricky Cheung's review (几乎全负面)

I cannot recommend this hotel. We were promised a 'premium' room, but our room smelled stale, as though someone had been smoking inside. It was also rather dirty. We called reception to report the problem, and were promised that someone would come and clean our room, but no one ever did. It was like talking to a brick wall. The beach was also dirty, and there were not enough sun loungers by the pool. When we arrived at the pool at 2 p.m., after check-in, they had run out of beach towels. So much for a holiday in the sun.

独立练习
Skill Builder 原文 + 题目

📄 SB Text 原文

[1] It seems fairly obvious that travel is good for us physically: whether we're strolling around a museum in a foreign capital or reading a good book on a sun lounger, a holiday offers us the chance to relax, de-stress from a hectic job or exams, and recharge our batteries. Increasingly though, experts are telling us that travel is also good for our mental well-being. [2] 'We are so busy in our daily lives—working, paying the bills, doing chores, looking after family members—that we don't have time to stop and think about what is really meaningful to us,' says Dr Barbara McCary, a psychologist. 'A holiday or—even better—an extended trip abroad gives us the chance to hit the "pause" button and think about what we want out of life and prioritize what really makes us happy.' Dr McCary says she has known clients who make dramatic career changes or even move to a different country after such overseas trips. 'And not one of them regretted it.' [3] Travel also offers the opportunity to reinvent yourself, especially if you are prepared to go solo. In a foreign place with no friends or family around you, no one knows who you are, what your job is, where you come from or your history. You start with a clean slate. Liam Thomas founded a successful travel company catering to solo travellers, Room for One, after a life-changing holiday alone. [4] Liam had a terrible motorbike accident just after he graduated from university, sustaining injuries to his lower legs. He was in hospital for six months and the rehabilitation process was long and hard. During that time, he lost a lot of friends and his world shrunk as he struggled to manage simple tasks he had once taken for granted. 'It probably sounds ridiculous, but the day I could put on my socks by myself, I was so happy!' he says with a grin. 'When I left the hospital, my confidence was at an all-time low,' he explains. 'My life was bleak and I knew I needed to do something dramatic to try to get my life back on track.' [5] As soon as he was able, Liam decided to travel around South America on his own. He was gone for just six weeks. 'Suddenly, I was in exotic surroundings, there was a language barrier and everything seemed alien to me. I was a bit hesitant, but I had to interact with the world around me, however confusing it seemed, and this gave me a new-found sense of wonder. It was like I was seeing things for the first time. My trip was relatively short, but it made me re-evaluate who I was as a person and what I believed in.' When Liam met new people at the first hostel he stayed at in Buenos Aires, he told them he was a student taking a gap year. 'They didn't know anything about my motorbike accident, so to them I wasn't "poor Liam" who had spent half a year in hospital. I was a curious twenty-something who wanted to know more about another country and culture. It was like being reborn. Now I feel like the world is my oyster.' [6] Going outside your comfort zone and planning a trip to a far-flung location or travelling on your own can seem a little bit frightening. When you actually do it, however, you might find that it is not as difficult as you expected. And even when things go wrong, travel can make you stronger emotionally, as you realize that you can handle situations that you might have shied away from at home. [7] Amelia Wong travelled around Europe for eight weeks last summer, on her own. She had to cope with missed trains, hotels being fully booked and having her passport stolen. 'It made me grow up,' she says. 'When my passport was stolen, I panicked at first. But then I gave myself a pep talk and managed to calm down. I went to the consulate and told them everything as calmly and confidently as I could. It all worked out in the end. At the time it seemed like the end of the world, but getting a replacement passport was actually not that much of a hassle.' [8] Francis Tam experienced every skier's nightmare when, travelling at break-neck speed down the slopes of Niseko, Japan, he fell and badly injured his knee. 'At the time it was a living hell,' he recalls. 'I had to call for help to be able to get to the medical centre, and I had an operation at the hospital in Japan. Then I had to rearrange my flight back home, because I couldn't bend my knee at all and needed three seats on the plane to stretch my leg out on.' Although the experience was awful, Francis says it has made him more resilient. 'If I'm ever in trouble again when overseas, I'll know what to do and it won't faze me.' [9] Even after a trip has ended, it can still have a positive impact on our mood and mental health. Studies have found that when we are on holiday, stress levels fall and we feel calmer. When we get back to our everyday lives, we can try to tap into those feelings of calm we experienced while away from home. [10] Joe Burrill, an expert in mindfulness, explains: 'One of the most relaxing holidays I have ever been on was in Boracay in the Philippines,' says Burrill. 'Now, if I feel like I'm starting to get a bit anxious about something, I just stop what I'm doing for 30 seconds, close my eyes and imagine I'm lying on that soft golden sand in Boracay. I take some deep breaths and try to remember what it felt like to be so relaxed. It helps me enormously when I need to calm down.' [11] If you were ever unsure, the evidence now seems as clear as day: travel is good for your mind, body and soul.

SB Q1 · Short Answer · 2 marks
What does the phrase 'Increasingly though' (line 4) suggest about the writer's attitude towards expert opinion on travel?
✅
参考答案
It suggests that the latest opinions are quite a recent development and/or that more and more people support this view.

[2] 'We are so busy in our daily lives—working, paying the bills, doing chores, looking after family members—that we don't have time to stop and think about what is really meaningful to us,' says Dr Barbara McCary, a psychologist. 'A holiday or—even better—an extended trip abroad gives us the chance to hit the "pause" button and think about what we want out of life and prioritize what really makes us happy.' Dr McCary says she has known clients who make dramatic career changes or even move to a different country after such overseas trips. 'And not one of them regretted it.' [3] Travel also offers the opportunity to reinvent yourself, especially if you are prepared to go solo. In a foreign place with no friends or family around you, no one knows who you are, what your job is, where you come from or your history. You start with a clean slate. Liam Thomas founded a successful travel company catering to solo travellers, Room for One, after a life-changing holiday alone. [4] Liam had a terrible motorbike accident just after he graduated from university, sustaining injuries to his lower legs. He was in hospital for six months and the rehabilitation process was long and hard. During that time, he lost a lot of friends and his world shrunk as he struggled to manage simple tasks he had once taken for granted. 'It probably sounds ridiculous, but the day I could put on my socks by myself, I was so happy!' he says with a grin. 'When I left the hospital, my confidence was at an all-time low,' he explains. 'My life was bleak and I knew I needed to do something dramatic to try to get my life back on track.' [5] As soon as he was able, Liam decided to travel around South America on his own. He was gone for just six weeks. 'Suddenly, I was in exotic surroundings, there was a language barrier and everything seemed alien to me. I was a bit hesitant, but I had to interact with the world around me, however confusing it seemed, and this gave me a new-found sense of wonder. It was like I was seeing things for the first time. My trip was relatively short, but it made me re-evaluate who I was as a person and what I believed in.' When Liam met new people at the first hostel he stayed at in Buenos Aires, he told them he was a student taking a gap year. 'They didn't know anything about my motorbike accident, so to them I wasn't "poor Liam" who had spent half a year in hospital. I was a curious twenty-something who wanted to know more about another country and culture. It was like being reborn. Now I feel like the world is my oyster.' [6] Going outside your comfort zone and planning a trip to a far-flung location or travelling on your own can seem a little bit frightening. When you actually do it, however, you might find that it is not as difficult as you expected. And even when things go wrong, travel can make you stronger emotionally, as you realize that you can handle situations that you might have shied away from at home. [7] Amelia Wong travelled around Europe for eight weeks last summer, on her own. She had to cope with missed trains, hotels being fully booked and having her passport stolen. 'It made me grow up,' she says. 'When my passport was stolen, I panicked at first. But then I gave myself a pep talk and managed to calm down. I went to the consulate and told them everything as calmly and confidently as I could. It all worked out in the end. At the time it seemed like the end of the world, but getting a replacement passport was actually not that much of a hassle.' [8] Francis Tam experienced every skier's nightmare when, travelling at break-neck speed down the slopes of Niseko, Japan, he fell and badly injured his knee. 'At the time it was a living hell,' he recalls. 'I had to call for help to be able to get to the medical centre, and I had an operation at the hospital in Japan. Then I had to rearrange my flight back home, because I couldn't bend my knee at all and needed three seats on the plane to stretch my leg out on.' Although the experience was awful, Francis says it has made him more resilient. 'If I'm ever in trouble again when overseas, I'll know what to do and it won't faze me.' [9] Even after a trip has ended, it can still have a positive impact on our mood and mental health. Studies have found that when we are on holiday, stress levels fall and we feel calmer. When we get back to our everyday lives, we can try to tap into those feelings of calm we experienced while away from home. [10] Joe Burrill, an expert in mindfulness, explains: 'One of the most relaxing holidays I have ever been on was in Boracay in the Philippines,' says Burrill. 'Now, if I feel like I'm starting to get a bit anxious about something, I just stop what I'm doing for 30 seconds, close my eyes and imagine I'm lying on that soft golden sand in Boracay. I take some deep breaths and try to remember what it felt like to be so relaxed. It helps me enormously when I need to calm down.' [11] If you were ever unsure, the evidence now seems as clear as day: travel is good for your mind, body and soul.
